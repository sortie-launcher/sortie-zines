The obvious heirs to the password are the codes modern games ask you to share, and many of them are not heirs at all. A Super Mario Maker course ID looks like a password: a short string you can print, post or read to a friend. But it carries no course. It is an address, and an address needs somewhere to point. Nintendo stopped course uploads for the Wii U game on 31 March 2021 and ended its online play on 8 April 2024. Every Wii U course ID still reads perfectly and leads nowhere.

A password carried its own payload. Nothing outside the cartridge and the player had to stay switched on. JUSTIN BAILEY decodes today exactly as it did in a German tip column in 1988, which is why it could be passed around for decades by people who never met.

The truer descendant is smaller. Pokémon Mystery Dungeon: Rescue Team DX, released in 2020, lets a player who has fainted in a dungeon ask for rescue online or "use an offline password system". Play Nintendo published a printable "Rescue Request Password Postcard" for it. The instructions: copy your password into the blank spaces, write your name, cut it out, and "share the postcard and your rescue request password with friends" so they can rescue you in the dungeon where you fell. Thirty empty boxes, in two rows, sit under the words "This rescue request password belongs to". The idea is older than the remake: the manual for the Game Boy Advance original, Red Rescue Team, already told players they could "use passwords to send and receive mail for friend rescue missions".

It is Metroid's CHECK BOARD and Dragon Quest's notebook, made into a postcard, with one change that matters. Metroid's manual presents the password as the way to "continue your old game". Readers mailed JUSTIN BAILEY to magazines anyway, magazines printed it, and the people who received it used it to reach a state they had never earned. Pokémon Mystery Dungeon makes that traffic the point and gives it a better reason: you are not handing a stranger a counterfeit, you are asking a friend for help. In this series the password has been addressed to someone since the Game Boy Advance. The postcard just gives it an envelope.
