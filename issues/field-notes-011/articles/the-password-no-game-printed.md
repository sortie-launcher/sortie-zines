The earliest printing of Metroid's most famous password that I could find on a scanned page is not American. It is on page 36 of Power Play, a German magazine then appearing as a special edition of Happy-Computer, in the issue dated 11/88. It sits in the Videospiel-Tips column, between a reader's Zelda II map and a cheat for the Thunder Blade arcade machine, in a column that opens with a Super Mario Bros. fireworks trick. Helge Heiner of Oldenburg, the column says, has found "einen tollen Cheat-Mode", a great cheat mode. Try this password: JUSTIN BAILEY, then a row of dashes. "Nun steuert man eine grünhaarige Frau, die alle erdenklichen Extras besitzt": now you control a green-haired woman with every extra imaginable.

Crop of a magazine scan. Power Play © Markt & Technik; scan via Internet Archive (powerplay_1988-11).](media/power-play-1988-11-metroid-tip.webp)

The string reached American readers eight months later. VideoGames & Computer Entertainment's Easter Egg Hunt for July 1989 credited it to Steve Bland of Kentucky and printed a photograph of the PASS WORD PLEASE screen. The magazine called it a tip to "turn Samus into a woman from the start", as though the password performed a transformation rather than showing who had been inside the suit. In November it ran a correction: the July hint was "a bit misleading", because the bottom row had to be filled with spaces or dashes. Nintendo Power, Nintendo of America's own magazine, got there in October 1991 with the headline "JUSTIN WHO?". A text search of the archived run finds nothing earlier, but the scans' OCR is patchy enough to miss real mentions, so treat that as a hint, not proof. Kate Willaert, who traced the American printings, reports that Bland told her he had not found the password himself.

So the code travelled as readers' mail, picked up and passed on, with an erratum four months behind it. To see why that is interesting, you have to read what the string says.

## What the string says

Metroid's cartridge password is 24 characters drawn from a 64-symbol alphabet. Each character carries six bits, which makes 18 bytes. Sixteen of them hold the game state. The seventeenth is a "shift" that scrambles the sixteen by rotating their bits, and the eighteenth is a checksum: the first seventeen added together. A community disassembly of the game shows what the sixteen bytes contain. There are flags for items collected and doors opened, the area to start in, a flag for Samus without her suit, her equipment, a missile count, the boss statues, and a four-byte play clock that later decides which ending you see.

I ran JUSTIN BAILEY and its twelve dashes through a re-implementation of those routines, not through the game. Samus starts in Norfair with her suit off, carrying every piece of equipment except the Ice Beam and 255 missiles, with Kraid and Ridley already dead. The dashes do most of the work. They set the last nine bytes of the raw string to all ones, the shift and checksum included, so the twelve letters in front only have to land on the right total. Type any twelve characters followed by twelve dashes and about one attempt in 256 will be accepted. Willaert's verdict, "nothing more than a coincidence", is the code's verdict too. The only password Metroid compares against a stored string is a different one.

![Original diagram: the 24 boxed characters of JUSTIN BAILEY followed by twelve dashes, and below them a bar of the 18 decoded bytes. Item and door flags, start area Norfair with the suit flag set, seven of eight items without the Ice Beam, 255 missiles, a play clock of FF FF FF FF, Kraid and Ridley beaten, shift 255 and a valid checksum of 255. A note explains that the clock's lowest byte never reaches 255 in normal play. Diagram by Sortie Field Notes from the metroidret/m1disasm community disassembly.](media/justin-bailey-decoded.webp)

The clock is the strange part. The dashes also fill Samus's play timer with its maximum value, and the game's own timer never counts that high: its lowest byte rolls back to zero at 208, so it can never read 255. No ordinary game reaches the state that JUSTIN BAILEY describes. The ending routine, which grades the clock, gives a value that large the lowest grade, and according to the code that ending puts Samus back in her suit. The password that made a suitless Samus famous ends, if you carry it to the finish, with the suit back on.

So JUSTIN BAILEY is not a save. Nobody earned it, and nobody could have. It is a sentence that the game's grammar accepts and its world never says.

## Other people's sentences

Metroid has exactly one password its programmers wrote in advance. If the first sixteen characters read NARPASSWORD00000, entered on screen as NARPAS SWORD followed by zeros, the game skips the checksum entirely and, on every frame, pins Samus's energy in the thirties, gives her every piece of equipment and resets her missiles to five. Fans read "NAR" as Toru Narihiro, who ported Disk System software to cartridge for Nintendo and is credited in the NES version's staff roll ("CONVERTED BY T.NARIHIRO"). It is a reasonable inference; I have not seen Nintendo confirm it.

Japan has its JUSTIN BAILEY in Dragon Quest II. A 52-kana Famicom password opens ゆうていみやおうきむこう, continues with ほりいゆうじとりやまあきら (Yuji Horii and Akira Toriyama, spelled out), and ends in a long run of ぺ. Fed to a community decoder, it produces a lone hero named もょもと with 942,197 experience points and 27,671 gold. The same state's ordinary password is 18 kana long; the other 34 characters are surplus the game happens to tolerate. ゆうてい is commonly glossed as Horii's pen name in Weekly Shōnen Jump's games column. Where the string was first published I could not establish, and claims that it was planted rest on hearsay.

The designers put locks on their passwords, but weak ones. The rotation and byte-sum checksum in Metroid catch most miscopied characters; they do not stop a determined forger. John David Ratliff, who documented the format, calls the checksum "a glib test" of whether the data is "likely to be what you meant to enter". And Nintendo of America itself published the methods. In Nintendo Power's November/December 1988 Classified Information, a tip credited to "Agent 705" told Deadly Towers players to let the prince die at once, write down the password, then "substitute the first two letters with EF or FE". The screenshots show 051K10.TDE becoming FE1K10.TDE, with the promise that "Prince Meyer will have powerful protection!" The next issue told Gauntlet players to enter one password for both players and get "two identical fighters". The platform holder's own magazine was teaching readers to edit and clone saves.

## A state is a text

I began this issue intending to call the password the only save file you could read aloud. That is wrong. Home computers already saved games to tape and disk, Nintendo's Family BASIC put battery-backed memory into a Famicom cartridge in 1984, and nothing stops a determined person from reading any save file. What was unusual about the console password is narrower and, I think, better: it made the game's own way back in into a field of text, and anyone could fill it in. You needed no tool, disk or editor. You needed a pencil, a source and the controller the game had already given you.

That changed what a magazine could hand you. Not your save, which is yours by definition, but a state nobody had played: a prince with armour he never found, a warrior copied in two, a green-haired woman with everything and a clock that never ran. The tip pages of 1988 and 1989 printed counterfeit progress and called it a discovery, and when one of them left the dashes out, a correction column put them back. A checksum can guard against your mistakes. It cannot guard against other people's ideas, and the most famous password of the era was one of those.
