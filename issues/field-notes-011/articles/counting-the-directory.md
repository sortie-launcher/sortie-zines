Nostalgia remembers a password era. Nintendo's own paperwork does not. The March/April 1989 Nintendo Power came with a bound insert, "The Complete NES Game Pak Directory", promising details on each game, including "whether or not it has a password feature". I counted its six pages from the scans. Of the 179 titles with a release date, the Password/Battery column marks 22 as Password and 3 as Battery: The Legend of Zelda, Zelda II and Ultima. The other 154 have nothing in that column.

The column is not perfect. Tecmo Bowl is blank, although its manual describes passwords, and some publishers' games, Tengen's Gauntlet among them, are not listed at all, so the true figure is a little higher. But the shape is plain. By Nintendo's own marks in spring 1989, about one dated NES game in eight had a password, fewer than one in fifty had a battery, and most were marked with neither. For those, switching off meant starting again.

The magazine's tip pages agree. A census of Classified Information across Nintendo Power's first seven issues, July/August 1988 to July/August 1989, finds about 88 tips, of which three involve a password: a Punch-Out!! "pass key", the Deadly Towers edit and the Gauntlet clone. None prints a Metroid, Kid Icarus or Mega Man 2 password. The magazine spent its pages on button codes, routes and maps.

Where passwords did appear, they were sold. Nintendo Fun Club News introduced Metroid and Kid Icarus in summer 1987 as games with "Password Memory", and that autumn ran a sidebar, "PassWord Paks": "You can come back and start where you left off—next day, next week, next year!" In July/August 1989 Nintendo Power's Mega Man II feature advertised "an awesome new password system that makes writing down and entering passwords a breeze". A password was a feature you put on the box, not the furniture of the era.

My guess about why memory inflates it, and it is only a guess: many of the games that had passwords were long ones, adventures built to be returned to, and long games are the ones people remember. The list is not all adventures; about a third of the 22 are sports or arcade games. But a password marked a game as one you were expected to come back to, and most of the library did not ask that of anyone.
