Dragon Quest's Famicom manual treats saving as handwriting. "あなたの旅は、想像以上に長いものです", page 38 begins: your journey will be longer than you imagine, too long to finish in a day. When you stop, go back to the king on the second floor of Radatome Castle and he will teach you the spell of resurrection, ふっかつのじゅもん. "その呪文を、紙にうつしてください": copy the spell onto paper. The king, it adds, will remember your name.

He will not, strictly. The name is inside the spell, along with experience, gold, equipment and items, and the paper does the remembering. Over the page there is a drawing of a girl writing in a notebook while the king points at her, and two further instructions. Check every character several times, because a single mistake means you cannot continue. And if something irreparable happens, reset and start again from the last spell you copied; at the very least, visit the king whenever you level up.

Read as a design, that is a save system with as many slots as the player has paper, and a rewind button. Enix shipped it in May 1986, more than a year before Metroid's password reached America.

## The stationery department

Other manuals designed their own paperwork. Metroid's American manual ends its main text with a "CHECK BOARD" on page 43: nine empty rows under DATE, LAST SCENE and PASSWORD. The Famicom Disk System original, a year earlier, had kept three Samus files on the disk itself, each one named by the player and deletable with a KILL MODE. On cartridge, Samus stopped being a file and became a line of writing you could keep anywhere.

![Metroid NES manual (1987), page 43, "CHECK BOARD": a blank table of nine rows with the columns DATE, LAST SCENE and PASSWORD. Manual page as reproduced by Nintendo. Metroid © Nintendo; image from Nintendo's digital reproduction of the original manual (CLV-P-NAAQE).](media/metroid-check-board.webp)

Mega Man 2's American manual says "You may write this down" and tells you to "keep track of where the bubbles are". The same dots on the 5×5 grid are チップ, chips, in Rockman 2's Japanese instructions. Tengen's Gauntlet manual reaches page 27 before admitting the cost of all this: "If your password doesn't work, make sure you copied it down correctly. Double-check the 'H' versus the 'K' and the 'S' versus the '5'." A password was half a program and half a clerical task, and the manuals wrote the job description for the clerical half.

## The players who missed it

Developers mostly remember the clerical half. In a 1993 interview with the original Phantasy Star team, Toru Yoshida said a password for Phantasy Star IV would have been "so long it would take two screens to write out–not the kind of thing you can just write down on a notepad". In the 2008 liner notes for Mega Man 9, a modern retro sequel, one developer pictured "some poor soul holding his head in his hands" over a miscopied code. Another said: "Too bad, passwords really evoke that Famicom-ness…"

The players' side is more surprising. At CEDEC+KYUSHU in 2018, as reported by Famitsu, Yuji Horii said that when Dragon Quest III replaced spells with battery saves, he expected players to be pleased. Instead, many complained that the spells had been better, because they let you keep several sets of data. A battery cartridge gave you three adventure logs; a notebook gave you as many as you were willing to copy. The inconvenient system had been the generous one.

## A medium, not a country

I expected the password to be a localization artifact: Japanese games saved to Disk System disks, and American cartridges got passwords. Metroid and Kid Icarus do fit that story. But The Legend of Zelda and Zelda II crossed from disk to cartridge with batteries, and the first Castlevania crossed with no save at all. In Japan, cartridges such as Dragon Quest and Rockman 2 used passwords at home.

Dragon Quest went the other way. Nintendo Fun Club News previewed Dragon Warrior in its Winter 1987 issue under the heading "How to get your password": you "have to get the Mantra of Resurrection", by asking "the King residing in the castle of Radatome". By March/April 1989, Nintendo Power's directory listed the forthcoming game as "Battery". The 1989 American manual states in a box on page 2 that "Your Dragon Warrior Game Pak has a battery in it", and its tips repeat the Japanese manual's warning in a different medium: "Dragon Warrior is not a game that can be played in one sitting." Neither magazine says why the save changed.

My reading is that the medium and each game's budget decided it. In June 1987 Computer Entertainer listed Zelda at $44.95, calling it "the first game to include a built-in, 5-year lithium battery for saving your place and character development in an adventure", and Metroid and Kid Icarus at $39.95 each as "Password Paks". According to a chip-level cartridge database, all three American cartridges use the same board class with the same memory; only Zelda's has a battery. Zelda's $44.95 also came with gold plastic and its own television campaign, and I found no source giving the cost of the battery alone.

The battery kept the fiction and moved the paper inside. Dragon Warrior's manual calls the save the "Imperial Scrolls of Honor", and offers "Copy A Quest" as "insurance against accidental erasure". Copying a save, the thing players had done by hand, became a menu option for three slots.
