## Why this one, why now

Most mech games ask for your hands: Armored Core's boost management, Virtual On's dashes. *Carnage Heart EXA* asks for your head and then takes your hands away. You design an Overkill Engine (OKE) part by part, write its behaviour as a flowchart of logic chips, send it into the arena, and watch whether your program survives contact with an enemy someone else programmed. It's Artdink's PSP continuation of the line that began with the original 1995 PlayStation *Carnage Heart*, and it may be the best mech game ever made for a cramped seat: your reflexes can be spent and your patience still enough.

In North America it's a PlayStation Network release. Natsume published it on March 19, 2013, for $29.99, and announced it for PSP and PS Vita. Natsume's launch post described it as building on the original with updated chips, a better interface, more OKE types, and something the series hadn't offered before: you can now pilot an OKE yourself.

## The two halves of an OKE

**Hardware.** Reviewers describe a familiar garage with unusually physical trade-offs. You pick a body type: walkers, tanks, vehicles and flyers all appear. Then you fit main and sub weapons ranging from rifles and shotguns to beam guns, mines, missiles and bombs. HonestGamers' reviewer walked through the knock-on effects. Thicker armour adds weight. More engine output lets you carry it but burns more energy. Running out of energy stops the machine, but carrying more fuel makes overheating more likely, and overheating badly enough destroys you.

**Software.** This is the game. An OKE starts as an empty shell that can't even walk forward until you program it to. You place command chips on a grid and wire them into a flowchart. A sensor chip might scan 400 metres ahead in a 90-degree arc. It has a *yes* exit and a *no* exit, so a few chips give you "turn until an enemy appears, then advance." A return chip loops the whole program, and the OKE reads through it several times per second. Put the chips in the wrong order and your machine stutters on the spot, caught in a loop that does nothing.

What makes this playable is that the battle screen shows your flowchart live, with a highlight running through each chip as it fires. When something goes wrong, you can usually see the loop it's stuck in.

Manual piloting is EXA's headline feature, and it's stranger than it sounds. Per the HonestGamers review, you don't get default controls: you program what each button does, including how far a direction press moves you. Digitally Downloaded added that even manual control needs more functions than the PSP has buttons, so some automation is always part of it. You can mix the two, with machines that mostly run themselves but take your input, or the reverse.

## The first session

Know what you're signing up for. Story Mode is a long, structured tutorial. Each lesson opens with a lecture, walks you through putting the concept into chips and parts, and sends you against test drones or a scripted opponent. Lessons are framed by visual-novel scenes between a wheelchair-bound student, Kyle, and his programming tutor, Chi Ling.

Fights are short. HonestGamers cites time limits of a minute or two, and early lessons are one-on-one before the game moves to three-on-three teams. The battlefields run on a physics engine, so a program that loses can win an identical rematch. HonestGamers' advice was to re-run a close loss once before rewriting anything.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** Few games are this purely systemic. A garage plus a programming language means you're tinkering with both what the mech is and how it thinks. It's also unusually interruptible. The loop of design, test, read the failure and change one chip splits into short pieces, and nothing about a battle needs fast thumbs.

**Friction.** Neither detailed review hides the costs:

- **The story is anime visual-novel framing.** Both reviewers found it better than expected, but it's still a lot of text between lessons. Digitally Downloaded warned that less patient players will tire of the stream of instruction before the game hits its stride.
- **The tutorial is long.** The HonestGamers reviewer reported about eight hours of Story Mode, and said you really do need all of it.
- **Battle Mode is the real game.** HonestGamers describes fifty preset three-on-three battles with almost every body and part open, plus a points shop. It assumes you learned everything in Story Mode.
- **Performance.** Digitally Downloaded reported lag in three-way battles on original hardware. If a crowded fight stutters on an emulator, the game itself may be the cause.

## Controls and versions

It's a menu-and-editor game, so a controller suits it. According to HonestGamers, the chip editor supports selection, copy and paste, macros and undo, so look for those tools before you rebuild a program by hand. Field Notes did not play-test it on any emulator for this issue.

The 2013 release also supported sharing designs and programs with other players, through an official upload site and tournaments run from other players' data. Those online features belong to their time; everything in this article works offline.

## A first-session plan

- **Don't skip lessons, and don't reach for the example solutions.** Digitally Downloaded notes that leaning on them limits you and skips the experience.
- **Keep paper handy.** Sketch your flowchart before you place chips. You can design on paper even when you can't play.
- **Session goal:** finish the opening lessons, then write your own seek-and-approach program in the shape described above. Scan, turn on *no*, advance on *yes*. Get it to beat a test drone without using an example.
- **Stretch goal:** add an ammo check. Fire weapon one until it's empty, switch to weapon two, then fall back to melee. When that works, you've learned the language.

## How it was received

EXA arrived quietly in the West. Metacritic shows no Metascore because too few critics reviewed it. Digitally Downloaded's April 8, 2013 review, credited to Clark A., called it cold, technical and demanding. It also found it unusually rewarding for patient players, thought the story more engaging than expected, and said more than fifty hours is realistic if you take to designing machines. It didn't publish a numeric score.

A November 19, 2015 reader review on HonestGamers, by user Robotic_Attack, played on Vita, gave it 5/5. Treat that as an enthusiast's account rather than critical consensus. It is, though, the most detailed English write-up of the chip system. The honest summary is a niche game with a thin critical record, and both detailed English reviews agree on what it asks of you.
