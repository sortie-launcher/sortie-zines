## Why this one, why now

Shiren has had bigger and more elaborate entries since, including a 2016 Western release on PS Vita, but the DS cartridge is the one to start with, and the reason is the design, not nostalgia. *Mystery Dungeon: Shiren the Wanderer* is Sega's 2008 Western release of Chunsoft's DS remake of the 1995 Super Famicom game, *Fushigi no Dungeon 2: Fūrai no Shiren*. Apart from an earlier fan translation, it was the first time this game was available in English. It's the foundational design that later Shiren games build on: thirty areas between a starting village and the Land of the Golden Condor, a small inventory, a hunger counter, and death that takes everything you were carrying.

If Brogue is your model of a fair roguelike, this is its console cousin from the same lineage of *Rogue*-inspired design. Each run has the same rules and a different map. Character levels reset, but warehouse contents and some relationships with the people along the route persist, alongside what you've learned.

## How it actually plays

Everything is turn-based. Each step, attack, pickup or item use is one turn, and every monster and character on the floor gets one action in reply. Health comes back as you walk. Fullness goes down as you walk. So food is both survival and inventory clutter. You move in eight directions, attack with melee weapons or arrows, and solve problems with a deep box of items: herbs, scrolls, staffs, and jars that hold other items.

Some of the best ideas came out of Chunsoft's stated goal of making a game in the spirit of *Rogue* that anyone could pick up. In a 2016 interview, Koichi Nakamura and Seiichiro Nagahata described jars as a deliberate trade-off. They add storage space, but a trap can break one and scatter its contents across the floor. The DS team reworked graphics, item placement and balance rather than doing a straight port.

Persistence is narrow and deliberate:

- **Death resets the run.** You go back to the start without your level, items or money.
- **Warehouses persist.** Most villages along the route have storage where items survive your death. Blacksmiths upgrade weapons. Long-term progress comes from building up a few pieces of gear across many runs, not from your character's level.
- **People remember.** GameSpot's reviewer noticed that characters respond to what happened on earlier runs. One traveller who blinds you early on can later become an ally if you help her. Three companions can join in the main game, and a fourth joins after the ending, alongside extra post-game dungeons.

The decisions that matter are the classic roguelike ones: when to test an unidentified scroll, whether to clear the whole floor for items or take the stairs while you're healthy, which jar to trust, and when to give up the run's best sword by banking it. GameSpot's review has a neat example. Paralyse the monster cutting off your escape, swap places with it using a switching staff, and it becomes a cork in the corridor between you and the pack chasing you.

## The first session

You start in a village and walk out onto a sunny forest road. The first floors look harmless. Koichi Sugiyama's score draws on East Asian instruments such as the shakuhachi, and the HUD on the lower screen shows floor, HP and level. Then, as GameSpot warned, a single early encounter can take most of your health, and you start doing the arithmetic.

One practical point from GameSpot's review: you can't save freely. It describes the system as suspend only, not a save you can reload after a mistake. Your choices stand.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** It's close to instant-on: there's no story to sit through before you're making decisions, and its depth comes from how items, monsters and terrain interact rather than from stat grinding. The run structure also respects limited time. A short session either ends in a lesson or banks something in a warehouse.

**Friction.** The 2008 critics weren't wrong about the costs:

- IGN's Jack DeVries found the turn-based rhythm stop-and-go and said the punishment made him wary of experimenting. Experimenting is supposed to be the fun part.
- GameSpot counted the tight inventory and hunger management as archaic, and the randomness as both exciting and frustrating.
- Nintendo Life's Damien McFerran called the plot lightweight and the visuals dated, and still described it as one of the purest RPGs available.

Players raised on modern roguelikes will recognise all of this, but the opening hours can feel unfair until you start banking gear.

## Controls and versions

This is a DS game with information on both screens: the map and route above, the dungeon and HUD below. Dual-screen handhelds and emulator layouts can show both screens at once or stack them; either way the top screen is worth keeping visible. Field Notes did not play-test any emulator setup for this issue.

Eight-way movement on a D-pad is where careless deaths start. Walk around the first village until your diagonal steps are deliberate.

The DS release also has *rescue quests*. If you fall, another player can rescue you by password, local wireless or the Nintendo Wi-Fi Connection, and you continue with your items. The Nintendo Wi-Fi Connection service has since closed, and rescues always depend on another player, so plan as if every death is final.

## A first-session plan

- **Start with Fay's Puzzles.** The first town hosts a set of fifty item-and-positioning puzzles from the beginning of the game. Do three before your first descent. They teach the item tricks the main game expects you to find yourself.
- **Treat run one as a scouting run.** The goal is not to win. Reach the first village that has storage, and deposit the best weapon or shield you've found.
- **Use unknown items only on your terms:** at full health, with an exit or an escape item in reach.
- **Keep a note of what killed you and on which floor.** The next run's plan starts from that line.

## How it was received

The DS release landed in the West in March 2008 to mixed reviews. Metacritic lists a Metascore of 69 from 27 critics. GameSpot's Austin Shau gave it 6/10 on April 3, 2008. He enjoyed the item variety and the tricks you can exploit, and he was put off by the permanent death system. Among the outlets Metacritic lists, 1UP scored it 83, GamePro 80 and GameSpy 70, and GameSpy called the game "punishingly, unrelentingly hardcore." IGN's review by Jack DeVries ran on March 4, 2008, and Nintendo Life's Damien McFerran reviewed it on April 1, 2008.

Its reputation grew afterwards. As Wikipedia's reception section summarises, 1UP ranked it the 15th-best DS game in 2011, Hardcore Gamer called it one of the finest roguelikes ever made in 2014, and IGN placed it eighth on a 2016 list of the best dungeon crawlers.
