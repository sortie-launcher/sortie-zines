## Why this one, why now

Some shooters are about fireworks. *Battle Garegga* is about decisions, and it may be the purest example of that kind of arcade game. Raizing developed it and Eighting published it in arcades in 1996; this article describes the original arcade release, the version Arcade Database dates to February 3, 1996. It looks like an ordinary vertical shooter with propeller planes, a gritty industrial palette and a pounding soundtrack by Manabu Namiki. Under that surface it runs a hidden difficulty economy, and almost everything you do pays into it. Programmer Shinobu Yagawa had worked on *Recca* before this, and his later work includes *Ibara*. Next to friendlier shooters of its era, Garegga is the one that argues back.

## Rank: the system under the system

Garegga keeps a hidden *rank* value that sets how hard the game is right now. The Shmups Wiki's breakdown lists what raises it:

- **Time**, at a rate set mostly by your autofire speed.
- **Every shot you fire**, weighted by ship, shot level and number of options.
- **Every item you pick up.**
- **Every special weapon you use.**

As rank climbs, more small enemies spawn, enemies take more damage before dying, they fire more often, their bullets move faster, and items fall off the screen faster. Left unchecked, the game turns into a much more frantic shooter by the late stages.

There's one way to lower rank: lose a life. The fewer lives you have in stock when you die, the bigger the drop. So the game rewards a player who stays lean. Experienced players deliberately stay under-powered, hold their fire when nothing is on screen, and die on purpose just before an extend, so that the last stages stay playable. A no-death clear is possible, but the wiki says it takes deep rank knowledge and very careful play.

Everything else feeds the same loop:

- **Medals** chain upward in value, from 100 up to 10,000 points each, as long as you keep catching them. Let one fall off the bottom of the screen and the next one drops back to base value.
- **Extends** come every 1,000,000 points. There's also a secret 1UP in Stage 3 if you let the mid-boss drive all the way to the end of its track and park.
- **Special weapons** use ammunition from fragments dropped by ground targets. Forty small fragments make one full stock. You can fire with a partial stock at reduced strength.
- **Options**, the support pods, cycle through five formations on the third button. Secret formations unlock when you deliberately let certain items fall off the screen before grabbing the next pickup.
- **Dying** throws out shrapnel that cancels bullets and damages enemies, and it drops a large shot pickup so you can partly rebuild.

The result is resource management dressed as a shooter. You're always trading score, lives and firepower against how angry the game is getting.

## The first session

The first stage is pleasant. That's the trick. Bullets are realistically coloured rather than neon, explosions throw debris, and the visual noise makes some incoming fire hard to read. Wikipedia notes the later Type 2 revision swapped many bullets for yellow dots for exactly that reason. The screenshot at the top of this article shows the problem.

In later stages, the rank you built up earlier shows up as denser, faster fire. The arcade original has no meter to tell you why; as the Shmups Wiki puts it, there's rarely direct feedback when you're doing something "wrong." Expect your first credits to end in places you don't understand. That isn't failure; it's the first lesson.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** Garegga rewards players who like to think about systems. A credit is a self-contained experiment. Your choices compound, the systems interlock, and a run can go wrong in many different ways, so failure teaches you something specific. That also makes it a good short-session game: one credit is one complete lesson.

**Friction.** In the 1996 arcade version, the system is invisible. M2's *Battle Garegga Rev.2016* for PS4 and Xbox One added an on-screen rank graph, extra information displays, options to recolour bullets or fix rank per stage, and mid-run saves. Push Square's Damien McFerran saw those additions as solving the game's best-known complaint. None of them are in the arcade original. There you learn rank the old way: by inference, or from notes like the ones in this article.

## Controls: what's documented

Garegga uses three buttons:

- **A** fires.
- **B** fires the special weapon.
- **C** changes the option formation.

On any modern setup, make sure C is actually mapped before your first credit.

The single most useful practical tip: **don't use turbo.** Garegga has its own autofire. Tap A at a rhythm and the game sets its autofire rate to match. Hold A and it keeps firing at the current rate, starting at a default of 8.6 Hz. Faster autofire raises rank faster. Hold the button and let the game handle it.

The game was made for a vertical monitor, so on a landscape screen it will appear as a tall picture with bars at the sides unless you rotate the display. Field Notes did not play-test it on any emulator or hardware for this issue.

## A first-session plan

- **Pick Wild Snail and select it by pressing A, B and C together**, or let the selection timer run out. That gives the faster, smaller-hitbox variant the wiki calls *Golden Bat*, a popular choice for a first clear because its piercing main shot simplifies bosses. The four bonus characters are locked behind a title-screen code in the arcade version, so leave them for later.
- **Hold A. Never turbo.**
- **Credit one:** reach Stage 3 and try for the secret 1UP by letting the mid-boss park.
- **Credit two:** keep three options or fewer, ignore medals you can't chain, and see whether Stages 4 and 5 feel calmer.
- **Think in two halves.** The wiki's beginner advice is to control rank through Stage 5, then power up fully, hoard lives and bombs, and stop worrying about rank for Stages 6 and 7.

## How it was received

Garegga charted in arcades and became a lasting reference point for the genre. According to Wikipedia's reception section, *Game Machine*'s March 15, 1996 issue listed it as the eighth most successful arcade game of the month. Three reviewers in Japan's *Sega Saturn Magazine* each gave it 8/10. Germany's *Maniac* scored the 1998 Saturn port 57% and later the PS4 release 81%.

Later writing was warmer. Hardcore Gaming 101 called it "an absolute classic" in 2012. *Famitsu* gave Rev.2016 32/40, and IGN Japan gave the PS4 version 8.9/10.

Push Square's Damien McFerran reviewed the Japanese Rev.2016 on January 2, 2017. He described the hidden rank as divisive: absurd to some players, perfect balancing to others. His verdict was that it remains "a defining release in the shmup canon," and that M2's version is the best way to play it.
