In May 1994 Frank Stajano started setting exams in Doom. A player who wanted one of his Doom Honorific Titles asked for a challenge, received it signed with PGP, and recorded the required levels as demo files for anyone to download and watch. Each challenge also came with a dance, a short string of turns and punches: one of the first read RRFL-FFFF, where L and R were quarter turns and F was a punch. The dance had to appear in every recording. "If your LMP is branded with your dance," the 1995 rules explained, "nobody can say that you stole somebody else's LMP that was floating somewhere on the Net."

It is a strange thing to ask of a recording. Nobody asks the subject of a film to wave at the camera to prove it was filmed. But a Doom demo is not a film, and Stajano's dance is the clearest statement I know of what kind of record it is: one that can prove everything about what was pressed and nothing about who pressed it.

## Four bytes a tic

Doom runs its world at 35 tics a second. When it records, it writes a 13-byte header (version, skill, episode, map and a few switches) and then, for every tic, the four values the engine itself takes from the player: forward speed, sideways speed, turn and buttons. That is 140 bytes for each second of play. There are no pictures in the file, no positions and no monsters. On playback the values are fed back in place of the keyboard, and the game happens again. It can happen again because nothing else varies. Doom's random numbers are read in order from a fixed table of 256 bytes, which goes back to the start for every new game. In id's source code, the comment above the two functions that read it asks: "Which one is deterministic?"

Two details in that source say a lot. The demo writer leaves out the field network games use to detect two machines drifting apart, so a demo that goes wrong carries on going wrong, silently, the marine obeying inputs meant for a different situation. And recording changes the game being recorded. After writing each command the engine reads it back and plays that instead, so the player's turning is rounded to the single byte the file keeps. id's comment: "make SURE it is exactly the same". To be recorded faithfully, you play inside the recording's resolution.

The shareware Doom 1.9 file carries three demos, the ones that play behind the title screen. I decoded them. DEMO1 is 5,026 tics of E1M5 on Hurt Me Plenty, 143.6 seconds, with 65 presses of fire and 32 of use. On more than half of its tics the forward speed falls between the keyboard's fixed steps, in a near-continuous spread that tops out at 50; of the ordinary controls in id's released code, only a mouse produces a spread like that. John Romero has said that he recorded every demo-loop demo in id's games until he left after Quake. If his recollection is right, the chart above is his hand on a mouse. The file itself cannot say whose hand it was.

## A label, not a guarantee

From version 1.4 on, the only identity a demo carries is one byte, the version, and id treated it as a label. The shareware demos were re-recorded for almost every release from 1.0 to 1.8. id never documented why, though engine changes that broke the old recordings are the obvious suspect. The 1.9 demos are the 1.8 demos, identical except for that first byte, changed from 108 to 109.

Players' collections were stranded in the same way. The Doom FAQ that id shipped with version 1.666 warned that the LMP Hall of Fame package "only works with v1.2". In June 1995 Simon Widlake announced that every recording on his competition site "that can be converted to version 1.9 format" had been converted, and that the site now took version 1.9 only. Even id's own released source code, stamped version 110, refuses those files: "Demo is from a different game version!" The demos outlived the program that made them. They still play because source ports rebuilt the old programs. dsda-doom keeps compatibility levels for the main old engines back to Doom 1.2, and Chocolate Doom promises accurate reproduction of the DOS versions, "including bugs". To stay faithful to the record, a port has to keep the original program's mistakes.

## A verdict from the machine

Inside the right program, the record is hard to fake. Widlake announced his "Competition" site, which became Compet-n, on 29 November 1994, noting that his first proposal had drawn "a total response of one (1) reply". By April 1995 his rules made the replayed game's own tally screen the official clock: "If the Time Taken can NOT be Clearly Seen - It'll be DISqualified !!!" The time came from running the game again, not from anyone's stopwatch.

Cheat codes act on the player directly, not through the four bytes, so a cheated demo stops matching once the cheat changes anything in the game. In 1996 Stajano deleted an exam in which one recording was supposed to use a cheat code, because "of course it doesn't replay"; he added that he was convinced nobody had meant to cheat. Uwe Girlich, who documented id's demo formats, put the property bluntly: if you change a single action in an LMP, "all the rest is garbage". When Quake arrived with demos that recorded the server's messages rather than the player's inputs, Stajano refused them the same standing, since the exam file "could be made up from scratch!"

## What the file cannot see

The four bytes record what the game received on each tic, not how long the player had to decide. Slow the game down while recording and the demo plays back at full speed with superhuman reflexes, perfectly valid. Compet-n's rules concede it: "it is impossible to 100% detect a slowmotion recorded demo". Detection meant tools, watching and "discussing by other players". After a new maintainer took over in 1997, the site disqualified two leading players over suspected slow motion. Its FAQ still says that "no one can be 100% sure" and that "a decision had to be made". The disqualified demos are still in the archive.

Stajano saw the far end of the problem early. Among the titles in his 1995 rules was Hacker: a player who "can write a program that writes out a LMP of itself getting to the end of a particular level". A demo is input to a program, and other programs can write it. Tool-assisted runs, built input by input, are the same idea carried out.

The dance, then, was a witness smuggled into the instruction: proof that a recording was made for one challenge, written in the only medium the file had. It guarded against a borrowed demo, not against slow motion or tools, and Stajano conceded that even its extra rule, killing two monsters after dancing, deterred only "all but the most skilled LMP forgers". The Doom Speed Demos Archive now holds more than 131,000 demos, each an exact account of what was pressed. Its rules count any demo that changes the game speed as tool-assisted. They say that edited game logic would desync, but on cheating claim no more than that "some types of cheating would be visible in the demo file itself". For the rest, it relies on what Compet-n relied on in 1997: rules, and other players.
