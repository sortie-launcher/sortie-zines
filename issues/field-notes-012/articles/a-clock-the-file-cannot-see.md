A replay from TrackMania Nations Forever or United Forever is several records in one file. It holds a copy of the track, samples of the car's position and speed for the ghost you watch, the game version and a checksum of the executable, and every input the player made, timed on the 100 ticks per second of the physics engine. The game's Validate button runs those inputs from start to finish and checks that the simulated car arrives where the recorded ghost did. "Trackmania's physics engine is completely deterministic," as the investigators of 2021 put it. "This means that the exact same inputs will always yield the exact same results."

Now run the game at 40 per cent of its normal speed. The inputs still land on the right ticks, the simulation still matches the ghost, and the run still validates. Played back at full speed, it shows the steering of someone who had two and a half times as long to think. Nothing in the file records how fast the game was running. In the report's words, slowing the game in offline play "does not reveal this fact to anyone that inspects the replay file".

## Spikes per second

On 20 May 2021 the community programmer donadigo and the YouTuber Wirtual published the "TMX Replay Investigation", covering every world record on the official tracks of both games at Trackmania Exchange, the community site where players upload replays. donadigo had "re-discovered" in 2019 that replays store all the inputs; the format had been documented on a community wiki but, as they put it, "never made use of in real applications". They extracted the steering from 19,977 United replays and 5,691 Nations replays and counted "spikes per second": how often analogue steering changed direction within each second, ignoring changes smaller than 2,000 units, about 1.5 per cent of the range. Another 13,618 United replays had to be left out because they were driven on an older version of United, which is "not compatible with the newest version in terms of physics". Even here, the instruction only ran on its own program.

The count was a filter, not a verdict. "The script is in no way a measure of judging replays," the report says, "it is instead a tool to target most suspicious players for deeper analysis." It flagged players the authors went on to clear. One extreme steering pattern could be reproduced with very high analogue sensitivity, and they argued that the player did not cheat.

What turned suspicion into an argument came from outside the replay. Well-known players were asked to move a stick as fast as they could, to measure what hands can do. The only way the authors could reproduce the suspect runs was to slow the game down and play the result back at normal speed. Some patterns appeared only offline: "Replays driven offline feature suspicious input sequences that cease to exist in online or on-stream play." For one suspect run, the exchange's own session and IP records showed which account had uploaded it. Then came confessions. The report relays that three players contacted the investigators privately and admitted using Cheat Engine, two of them saying they slowed the game, one to speeds of "40%-80%". It argues that seven logins used the technique. I found no public admission from the other four, and I am not naming any of them here.

## The macro and the official count

A week after the report, the holder of the celebrated A01 record published a confession about a different cheat. The report had not named him. On that track, trabadia wrote, he had used a macro to hold steering angles through the drift: "the real human record of A01 is not 23.83." His best time without it, he said, was 23.84. And he noted that the macro "doesn't synchronize well with TM so it always gave different results". A deterministic game is hard to cheat consistently even when you are the one cheating.

On 13 July 2021 Florent Castelnérac, the head of Nadeo, the studio behind the series, posted: "We ran anticheat detection for 400k records made over the last 15 years on TMUF." The cheated records would be removed, and "the majority were own by 10 logins". The number of fair records, he wrote, was "above 99%". Nadeo named nobody and did not describe its method; keeping quiet, he wrote, "is best for anticheat development".

## A witness for the clock

The remedy was to add what the file lacked. donadigo had published the TrackMania Competition Patch in December 2020, and by the time of the report the exchange already required it for important leaderboards: players had "to install the patch for their replays to be approved". It "overrides the input system of TrackMania so that it can verify that all inputs come from a real hardware device," and it "saves its own metadata about all replays driven by players to detect input injection methods and game speed modification." It is Stajano's dance again, built as software: a witness for what the inputs cannot show.

The replays were never wrong. Each was an exact account of what the car was told to do on every tick. The clock lied, and the clock was the one thing the file had no reason to keep, because to the game, the game's time is the only time there is.
