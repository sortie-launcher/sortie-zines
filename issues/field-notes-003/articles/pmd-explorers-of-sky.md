## Why this one is here, and the honest warning

*Pokémon Mystery Dungeon: Explorers of Sky* (DS, North America October 2009) is Chunsoft's Mystery Dungeon engine wrapped in the most heavily narrated, most child-friendly presentation in the whole lineage. It's in this issue because it represents a different roguelike tradition: party management, recruiting and story, with *light* failure costs. It's also very popular. Many fans now treat it as a cult classic and one of the best Pokémon games.

The warning, plainly: of the four games here, this is the one most likely to lose a roguelike veteran in its first hour. The opening is all dialogue, the tutorials are long, the tone is relentlessly earnest, and the dungeon tension is mild until much later. Read on if you want a low-stakes, comforting crawl for a tired evening, or a gentle first roguelike for a younger player. Skip it if you want Brogue-style dread.

## What Sky actually is

Sky is the enhanced third edition of 2008's *Explorers of Time* and *Explorers of Darkness*. IGN's Jack DeVries complained at launch that it has the same plot, the same characters and the same dungeons. Here's what Sky adds, according to Wikipedia and the reviews:

- **Five Special Episodes**, unlocked as the main story progresses and played from the title menu, starring Bidoof, a young Wigglytuff, Sunflora, Team Charm and Grovyle. 1UP's Jeremy Parish called them the game's saving grace. They're self-contained and tighter, and "at times, they almost feel like a real roguelike."
- **Spinda's Café**, a town hub for drinks, item recycling, extra missions and new optional dungeons.
- **Lookalike items** that punish careless reading. An Oren Berry *damages* whoever eats it, unlike the healing Oran Berry.
- **Five new starters** (Phanpy, Riolu, Shinx, Eevee and Vulpix) and a post-game Shaymin Village and Sky Peak. Wikipedia notes that Sky Peak deliberately echoes Shiren's Table Mountain, with a village-to-summit climb, checkpoints and a boss at the top.

## How it actually plays

A personality quiz decides which Pokémon you become. You then choose a partner, wash up on a beach with amnesia, and join Wigglytuff's exploration guild. You take jobs from the board, go into randomly generated dungeons, and look for the stairs, the target item or the Pokémon you've been sent to rescue.

Combat is Mystery Dungeon's simultaneous turns on a grid. Moves come from Pokémon, have limited PP per dungeon, and follow the main games' type chart. You only control the leader. Team members follow AI tactics you set at the guild, and the manual suggests switching off individual moves so allies save PP for strong enemies. A Belly meter empties as you explore, and once it's empty you lose HP. Gummis raise IQ, which unlocks AI skills. Defeated enemies sometimes ask to join, and as Nintendo Life's 2016 review complained, recruiting is mostly luck.

## What failure costs, and why that changes everything

The manual is clear: if *you or your partner* are defeated, the team goes back to the guild or the last save point. You **keep your levels and moves**, but lose **half your team's money and some carried items**. Choosing Give Up costs the same. Wikipedia's summary of the Explorers games describes a harsher version (all money and half or more of your items) and says that after the main story only the leader fainting ends a run. Sky's manual, quoted above, is the primary source here.

RPGamer's Glenn Wilson called it "by far the most inconsequential punishment" in the subgenre. 1UP said that outside boss fights and optional special dungeons you're rarely in danger. So PMD isn't really a test of survival. It's a test of patience: grinding gear, recruiting, and building IQ through a long campaign.

That reframing matters. Treat Explorers of Sky as a gentle, story-driven run of chores, with optional sharp edges in its special and post-game dungeons. It isn't a deathtrap.

## The first session

Expect a quiz, a beach, and a first errand to recover your partner's Relic Fragment from some bullies. After that comes a guild routine that repeats day after day: job board, dungeon, return, cutscenes. RPGamer described the opening at length as a "deluge" of text bubbles, many of them repeated daily, and noted that much of the dialogue can't be skipped. Nintendo Life's Mitch Vogel liked the story, but still called the text "mountains". There's no way to fast-forward through this part.

## A first-session plan

- **Take the quiz honestly, or not at all.** It only decides which Pokémon you play. Your choice of partner matters more for type coverage.
- **Pick a partner that covers your type weaknesses.** RPGamer points out that being weak to a dungeon's type is what makes the game hard.
- **Set the top screen to the map or text log** in Options. Leave the controls reference up only until the shortcuts are muscle memory.
- **Bank Poké at the Duskull Bank and items at Kangaskhan Storage before every dungeon.** The manual says banked money is safe even if you're defeated, so this cuts the failure cost to almost nothing.
- **Practise at the Marowak Dojo.** The manual describes it as a place where defeat costs neither items nor money.
- **Learn to quicksave properly.** In a dungeon it makes a temporary file that's deleted once you resume, so save again before you stop for long. The guild bed is the permanent save.
- **Read item names letter by letter** now that lookalikes exist.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** Short dungeons, a tidy turn grid, and gentle stakes suit a sleepy late-evening session. The Special Episodes and Sky Peak bring back some of the tighter roguelike structure that genre fans look for.

**Friction.** There's a lot, and it isn't subtle:

- **Story and anime tone.** It's a children's game about friendship and courage, told through very long, sincere dialogue. Players who dislike slow JRPG pacing will feel it here more than anywhere else in this issue. RPGamer was scathing about the repeated speeches. NZGamer called it "a bizarre mix of child-like presentation with hardcore gameplay mechanics".
- **Tutorial burden and low tension.** The early hours are tightly guided and easy. RPGamer rated the challenge "Very Easy", and even its defenders talk about grind.
- **One unit to control.** 1UP felt like an observer rather than a participant.
- **Recruitment luck and repetition.** Nintendo Life described the whole game as close to "one massive grind".

**Fan-made alternatives.** Two community projects rework the same engine family, and both are fan hacks rather than official releases. *Rogue Rescue Team* by FourBolt rebuilds the GBA *Red Rescue Team* as a seeded ten-dungeon roguelike gauntlet that reveals one challenge at a time, with Normal, Hard and Nightmare modes that shrink carrying capacity and recruiting. *Explorers of the Spirit*, by MaxSchersey and team, is a full-length new story on the Explorers of Sky engine; SkyTemple's hack directory describes redesigned dungeons, no traps or random monster houses, and no item loss on failure, which makes it gentler still. Field Notes did not play-test either hack.

## Controls: what's documented

According to the controls screen and the manual, everything works with buttons: running, set moves, turning on the spot, waiting and the message log. Touch input is optional. The DS layout puts the top screen to work as a map, text log or controls reference, so dual-screen handhelds and emulator layouts that show both screens at once suit it well. Field Notes did not play-test it on any emulator or hardware for this issue.

## How it was received

Launch reviews were cool. Metacritic lists a Metascore of 54 from 26 critics: 2 positive, 15 mixed, 9 negative. IGN's Jack DeVries (13 October 2009) gave it 4.9, calling it lazy, dated and "a rip off" for anyone who owned Time or Darkness. 1UP's Jeremy Parish (27 October 2009, C+, listed as 58) praised the Special Episodes and recommended Shiren on DS for players who want a real challenge. Nintendo Power's 80 (December 2009) and Official Nintendo Magazine UK's 70 ("a surprisingly tough game") were among the few warmer scores. Cubed3 gave 60 and pointed readers towards "purer" roguelikes like Shiren. Wikipedia records Famitsu's score as 35/40.

Later reassessment has been warmer. Nintendo Life's review of the 2016 Wii U Virtual Console release by Mitch Vogel (12 September 2016, 7/10) praised the story and charm and warned about the grind. Wikipedia describes the fan reputation as a cult classic, which is a community verdict, not a contemporary critical one.
