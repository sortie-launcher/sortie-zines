## Why this one, and which Baroque

*Baroque* is the roguelike where dying moves the story forward. Sting Entertainment first released it on the Sega Saturn in 1998, ported it to the PlayStation in 1999, and remade it for PlayStation 2 in 2007. Atlus brought the PS2 remake to North America in April 2008, Rising Star Games released it in Europe that August, and that was the West's first official version. Each death sends the nameless protagonist back outside the Neuro Tower without his levels or items, and the townspeople have new things to say. Sometimes a death is exactly what the next part of the story requires.

The remake also appeared on Wii, and GameSpot reviewed the two versions together; this article doesn't cover the Wii controls. Beyond that, English-speaking players have two quite different ways in:

- **The PS2 remake, *Baroque* (2008, Atlus in North America).** The official localisation: fully voiced in English, third-person, analog movement, with adjustable difficulty according to Wikipedia. It's the edition this article is built around.
- **PlayStation, *Baroque: Yuganda Mousou*,** with Plissken's English fan translation. Romhacking.net announced it on 26 July 2022, and its entry now lists version 1.0.2 with a date of 7 August 2022. This is the 1999 PlayStation port of the Saturn original: first-person with tank controls.

The Saturn original has no English release, and this article doesn't cover it.

## How it actually plays

You start outside the tower with nothing. Wikipedia's summary and the reviews agree on the core loop. The Archangel gives you the Angelic Rifle, a gun that kills any enemy in one shot but holds very little ammunition. RPGFan's Dennis Rubinshteyn counted five bullets. Each floor of the Neuro Tower is generated at random. You look for the portal down, fight or avoid former humans who have become Meta-Beings, and pick up equipment, flesh, hearts and odder items.

The survival layer is two meters:

- **HP and VT (vitality).** VT drains all the time. While you have VT, it feeds your HP. When VT runs out, your HP starts draining instead. Eating flesh restores HP and eating hearts restores VT. Eat either one while that meter is already full and its maximum goes up. Hunger, healing and permanent growth are all the same decision.
- **Throwing.** Every item can be thrown, so the items that would hurt you are often more useful thrown at something else. GameSpot's Lark Anderson called this the boon that rescues a thin melee system. You carry up to twenty item types. The reviews also mention fusing weapons with items for new effects, and enemy elemental weaknesses you can exploit.

## What dying costs, and what it buys

Death takes away everything you carried and every level you gained. You wake up again in the ruined Overworld. Rubinshteyn's review spells out how the game softens that: levels come quickly, you can save once you've stepped onto a floor's portal, and you can bank items by throwing them into Consciousness Orbs. Each orb transfers one item to the surface, where the Collector stores it. RPGFan explains that you get your items back from him by hitting him, and that he starts with five slots and gains more when you give him items he hasn't identified.

The unusual cost is narrative. Reviewers agree that the game won't tell you how to move the story along. Some characters and areas only appear after a death, sometimes a death in a particular place. Rubinshteyn put it neatly: clearing the tower isn't enough, and you have to work out when it's a good time to die. As key story moments trigger, the tower gets deeper. You won't find an objective list. IGN's Daemon Hatfield singled out a training dungeon run by the Coffin Man that the game never points you towards.

That makes Baroque a distinct tradition. Shiren treats death as a lesson. Baroque treats it as a key.

## The first session

Expect to be lost, and treat that as the opening rather than as a failure. GameSpot noted that some story terms are only explained in the manual and that the protagonist's guilt is asserted rather than shown. IGN complained that the map doesn't even make it clear where the tower is. The tone is bleak, mannered post-apocalypse, not anime comedy. That will suit some players better than the cheerful chatter of the PMD article later in this issue, but it is still cryptic Japanese storytelling, and every line is voiced.

## A first-session plan

- **Find the Coffin Man's training dungeon before anything else.** IGN said nothing points you to it, and RPGFan described him as the tutorial character. Learning the attack timing and throwing there costs you nothing.
- **On your first tower descents, scout.** Don't hoard. Pick up items to learn what they do, and throw anything that is obviously harmful at an enemy.
- **Save the rifle.** Five shots in reserve are your emergency exit from a fight you can't win.
- **Watch VT before HP.** If VT is falling and you have no hearts, start looking for the portal now.
- **Talk to everyone after every death, and keep notes.** The story responds to how and where you died. Paper notes beat memory between sessions.
- **Save on portals.** The portal save is your natural stopping point for a short session.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** The run design is persistent and tied to discovery, a bit like the lore unlocks of Caves of Qud, but the knowledge you need lives in the world rather than in a manual. Items pull double duty, thrown or used. VT works as a clock you can extend. And the game isn't padded: it never makes you sit through hours of introduction before the first real decision.

**Friction.** It's substantial, and the critics agree about most of it:

- **Combat.** GameSpot found it mostly running away and button mashing, with no block or dodge and only one attack that's really effective. RPGFan called it slow and a little unresponsive, worst when you're bare-handed.
- **Camera.** GameSpot called it almost entirely manual and endlessly frustrating in a fight: lock-on doesn't turn the camera towards your target.
- **Opacity.** If you dislike games that hide their structure, Baroque does it deliberately. Repeated floors drag once you can't work out the next trigger, and RPGFan recommended short sessions.
- **Presentation.** Muddy textures, a grain filter that sometimes obscures the map, and only a few floor themes.

**The PS1 alternative.** Its first-person view and tank controls trade the PS2 camera problems for older control friction. A forum poster announcing the translation claimed the PS1 port is easier than the Saturn game, adds the Collector, and has more floors. Wikipedia confirms the lower difficulty and changes to floor generation in that port. If the PS2 combat puts you off, the translated PS1 disc is a stranger and possibly purer experiment, but it gets no contemporary English reviews in this issue because there weren't any.

## Controls and emulation: what's documented

The PS2 remake uses analog movement and a manually controlled camera, so it needs both sticks. RPGFan's review mentioned frame-rate dips when a lot happens on screen, on original hardware. Field Notes did not play-test either the PS2 remake or the translated PlayStation disc on any emulator or hardware for this issue.

## How it was received

Western reviews of the 2008 remake were divided, and the criticisms are consistent. Metacritic lists the PS2 version at 60 from six critics. IGN's Daemon Hatfield (30 April 2008) gave it 5.4: unique in concept, lacking in fun, more convoluted than refreshing unless you're a hardcore roguelike player. He also admitted it "is not as terrible as it initially appears to be". GameSpot's Lark Anderson (16 April 2008) gave it 6.5 and wrote that it is at times "an incredible work of interactive fiction" and at others "a muddled mess". RPGFan's Dennis Rubinshteyn (April 2008) gave it 78% and said only players who truly appreciate roguelikes would get the most out of it. Metacritic also lists GamesRadar+ at 70, Cheat Code Central at 56 and PSX Extreme at 48. Wikipedia records Famitsu's scores as 25/40 for the 1998 Saturn original and 27/40 for the PS2 remake.

The original Saturn and PS1 releases never came out in English, so this issue has no English-language contemporary reviews of them. The translation's reception is limited to fan discussion, which is not critical consensus.
