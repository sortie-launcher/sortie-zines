## Why this one opens the issue

Every roguelike decides what survives a failed run. Brogue keeps nothing but what you learned. Shiren keeps a warehouse. Konami's *Azure Dreams* (PlayStation, North America 1998) chose a strange answer: Koh, the hero, keeps almost nothing, but the monsters he raises keep everything.

That makes it the most obviously systemic game in this issue and the most dated one. It also suits short sessions better than its reputation suggests: the tower saves between floors, a good climb can stop at any elevator, and the long-term project of raising familiars and tempering one sword moves forward a little on almost every trip up the tower.

Expect friction too. There's a dating subplot, a famously rough translation, and a forty-floor tower that uses one musical theme throughout. This article covers which parts are worth your time and which you can ignore.

## The rule that defines everything

The town of Monsbaiya sits under the Monster Tower. Koh turns fifteen, inherits his vanished father's trade as a monster tamer, and starts climbing. The tower is forty floors of randomly arranged rooms and corridors on a turn-based grid: when you take a step, everything on the floor takes a step. Floors 2 and 40 are fixed, and so is a scripted tutorial floor you only see once, but everything else is regenerated on every visit.

Then comes the defining rule. In the words of Electromax's long-running GameFAQs survival guide, whenever Koh leaves the tower, whether by Wind Crystal, by sacrificing a familiar with an Oleem, or by defeat, **his level resets to 1. His familiars keep theirs.** The guide sums up the loop in six steps: climb, level Koh and the familiars, leave when things turn bad, accept Koh's reset, spend the money in town, repeat.

So character growth is split three ways:

- **Familiars** hatch from eggs found in the tower and keep their levels between trips. Eggs only contain monsters from around the floor you found them on, so getting higher means finding better eggs. Once you have both fusion collars, two familiars can be fused, and fusion passes on genus, spells and up to two traits. That fusion system is the deepest part of the game.
- **Equipment** improves with Red and Blue Sands, which permanently add attack or defence. Invisible Rust traps take points away again. The standard advice in the guide is to commit early to one sword and one shield. Diamond Shields and some other gear don't rust, and the guide pairs a Diamond Shield with a Gold Sword. Pour every Sand into those two pieces and you are effectively levelling up without levelling.
- **The town** grows with your money. GameSpot's 1998 review listed a theatre, a racetrack, a bowling alley, a casino and a library among the buildings you can fund. According to Wikipedia's summary, some buildings bring in love interests, and others add minigames or ways to earn money faster. The sources treat the town as a side economy, not the main way to get stronger.

## Consequences and costs

Here is what you're risking. Koh can carry twenty items, but the tower door turns him away if he arrives with more than five, and familiars count against that limit. The guide's default loadout is two familiars, a sword, a shield, and one free slot for a Wind Crystal or food. If Koh dies, he wakes up in town without the equipment he carried. RPGFan's Andrew DeMario, reviewing in 2000, warned of a second cost: dying in the tower also autosaves, so there's no reloading your way out of it.

The tower adds pressure of its own. According to DeMario, if you linger on a floor too long it collapses and forces you upward, and thieving monsters can make off with an item before you can get it back. Monster Den traps drop a room's worth of enemies around you. The guide says it only met them three or four times, all between floors 34 and 39, and in each case it usually meant reaching for a Wind Crystal. Eggs pose a smaller dilemma: hatch one in the tower and the familiar only lasts until you leave. Hatch it at home and you keep it.

The result is a real risk-and-reward roguelike, closer to an extraction game than to Shiren. Every floor asks the same question: is what you're carrying worth one more elevator?

## The first session

The game starts slowly. There are birthday scenes at home, an angel naming sequence, and then the town. Go straight to the tower. On the first floor Kewne, the talking familiar who becomes Koh's partner, guides you through items, combat and movement. After that one floor, the tutorial never comes back.

The early floors are cheap to lose, and the fusion system pays you back for paying attention. Watch how monsters steal, how rust affects equipment, and which eggs show up where. GameSpot's James Mielke noted that you can change each familiar's AI at any time. The guide recommends keeping one out at a time, setting a weak one to *Follow Me*, and keeping its MP in reserve until roughly floors 10 to 12. In a bad moment, a familiar can also be used as a living shield.

## A first-session plan

- **Skip the dating subplot.** Sources describe a dating sim with seven possible love interests, and it's optional. Set it aside unless curiosity gets the better of you.
- **Finish the tutorial floor, then plan your exit before you plan your loot.** Your first priority on any trip is a Wind Crystal. Without one, a bad floor costs you everything you're carrying. Keep spares in your safe at home.
- **Hatch eggs at home, not in the tower.** Even a weak familiar is fusion material later.
- **Choose your sword-and-shield project early.** Stop swapping gear and pour Sands into the two pieces you'll keep.
- **Save at an elevator before you stop playing.** The elevator is the only save point in the tower, and a Go Up trap moves you to the next floor without offering one.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** The interactions are real. Fusion inheritance, AI-controlled familiars, rust against Sands, and a five-item entry limit that makes every trip a packing decision: this is design where planning beats grinding. Because the familiar stable carries over, short sessions still add up.

**Friction.** Most of the period complaints still stand:

- RPGFan scored the translation as among the worst DeMario had seen and called the music a single theme remixed across a two-to-three-hour climb. Bring your own audio.
- GameSpot found the visuals messy: pixelated sprites on rotatable polygon floors, with walls that turn transparent and an awkward overhead view.
- Next Generation doubted the random floors would bring many players back. GamePro's Boba Fatt pointed to repetition and luck-driven monster evolution as the main problems, while still calling the game addictive.
- Monster Dens, Rust traps and egg drop rates are unpredictable. Fusion gives the game its long-term direction, but drops still decide how quickly you progress.

Story and anime friction is lighter than in the PMD article later in this issue. The cutscenes are short, but the dating content is unavoidable in town even when it's optional.

## Controls: what's documented

The source material describes movement and attacks on an eight-direction grid, with a rotatable camera. On a D-pad, diagonal steps and camera angles are where mistakes happen, so practise in town first.

This article describes the North American PlayStation release. Field Notes did not play-test it on any emulator or hardware for this issue, so nothing here speaks to performance or controller mapping on a particular setup.

## How it was received

Coverage from 1998 was mixed to warm. GameSpot's James Mielke (22 July 1998) called it an RPG that was equal parts SimCity, Monster Rancher and role-playing game, praised its nonlinearity and found the presentation rough. Wikipedia's reception table lists GameSpot's score as 7.3. IGN's Steven Frost (August 1998, 7/10) compared it unfavourably with Suikoden and Vandal Hearts, but singled out the random tower and familiar breeding as real innovations. According to Wikipedia's reception table, Electronic Gaming Monthly's four reviewers gave 8, 8, 8 and 7, Game Informer gave 8/10, GameFan 86%, Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine 3/5, Next Generation 2/5, and Famitsu 29/40. GameRankings averaged 72%.

RPGFan's DeMario review came out in August 2000, two years after release, so it is effectively a retrospective. It scored the game 74% overall and 86% for gameplay, and called it more addictive than it had any right to be.
