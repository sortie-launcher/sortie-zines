## The shooter people remember for its speed

Soldier Blade has a reputation as one of the PC Engine's best vertical shooters, and most of that reputation is built on the first stage: fast, loud, colourful and generous. This article is about the parts that are easy to miss when the opening feels this good: the power-up rules as the manual states them, the score-attack mode the game was built around, and the tournament cartridge Hudson made for it.

Hudson released Soldier Blade on HuCard in Japan on July 10, 1992, and the TurboGrafx-16 version followed that September. It's the fourth Star Soldier game and the last of the PC Engine HuCard entries, with seven stages, which the game calls operations, each with a mid-boss and a boss. It rewards a second look because every power-up you collect is also your armour and your bomb stock.

## What the manual actually says

Konami, which now owns Hudson's catalogue, publishes the English manual from the PC Engine Mini release. It explains the system more exactly than most players' memories do.

- **Three capsule colours, three weapons.** Red is the Cluster shot, a vulcan that spreads into angled streams. Blue is the Laser, which widens into a central beam with side lasers. Green is the Wave, which stacks arcs across the screen. Each weapon has three power levels.
- **Same colour levels up. A different colour switches weapons without losing anything.** The manual says that taking a capsule of a different colour changes your attack, but your power level and "resistance level" stay the same. So if you're carrying a level-3 laser and grab green, you now have a level-3 Wave.
- **Power is armour.** Getting hit drops your power level by one instead of killing you. The Shmups Wiki adds that a hit at level 1 costs a life. GameSpot's 2007 review describes the same rule.
- **Every capsule is also a Super Shell.** You can hold up to three, shown as the shell stock in the bottom-right corner. Button I fires one. Each Super Shell lasts a fixed time, and your ship is invulnerable while it runs. The colour decides what happens: red's Maximum Heat Trace turns your escort pod into a flaming projectile, blue's Sweeper Blade fires a huge forward beam, and green's Chase Blaster sends an energy bomb flying around the screen hunting enemies.
- **The pod sticks around while you have shells in stock.** The manual calls the escort the Gunbody option unit and notes that it also destroys enemy missiles.
- **Burst Out.** Take a capsule of your current colour while you're already at level 3, and the overflow damages enemies. The Shmups Wiki describes it as a screen-clearing bomb.

The Shmups Wiki also says that spending a Super Shell doesn't reduce your weapon level. That's where the system gets interesting.

## The shell economy

With those rules together, a capsule on screen is a real choice. Do you take it to change weapon for the next section, take it to bank a shell, or take it as a Burst Out because you're maxed and the screen is busy?

The Shmups Wiki's survival advice is to max out one weapon, then fire a Super Shell every time a new capsule appears. You stay at full stock, and each shell gives you a few seconds of invulnerability. Played that way, Soldier Blade is about timing your capsule pickups so your invulnerable windows cover the worst moments.

There's a cost, and that's what makes it a system. Firing shells constantly means you're always one stock from empty. Your option pod leaves when the stock runs out, and so does its missile cover. Switching colours protects your power level, but the shell you get is in the new colour. A blue Sweeper Blade and a green Chase Blaster solve different problems. If you already know the stages, try picking colours for the next boss's shape rather than for the stage you're in.

Speed is another lever. Select changes your ship's speed during play, and the setting shows under your score: it reads LOW in the lead screenshot. The Shmups Wiki lists two speeds and a 1992 EGM write-up says three, so check for yourself.

## Caravan: the game it was built to be

Soldier Blade was made for a tournament. In a 2012 interview translated by shmuplations, designer Ukiuki Uribo says the project began as a discussion about which game to use for that year's Hudson Caravan, with about six months to finish it. The Caravan mode was "almost entirely the work" of designer Atsushi Kakutani. Graphic designer Hideki Yamaguchi adds that the game started as a 6-megabit card and was cut down to 4 megabits, and that the Caravan stage design was heavily reworked in the process.

The US manual calls it Challenge Mode. There are two versions: two minutes, meant for preliminary rounds, and five minutes, meant for championships. The rules change in ways that make it almost a different game:

- **Only the Cluster weapon can power up.**
- **No Super Shells, no option pod and no Burst Out.**
- **Capsules collected at level 3 become bonus points.**
- **In the five-minute mode, killing the last leader adds your remaining time and ships to your score.**

The manual also tells you to find hidden items and points-rich enemies yourself. The Shmups Wiki adds that shooting certain enemies at the same time with the multi-direction vulcan earns bonuses, and that the stages hide secret bonuses in shootable spots.

Hudson also made **Soldier Blade Special Version**, a limited PC Engine cartridge for the Caravan. Wikipedia describes it as containing only the two time-attack modes. Its title screen reads "Caravan Stage," with a timer running during play. It's the purest form of the score-attack game, and a separate release from the standard HuCard.

## A return-visit plan

- **Run 1, Normal Game on Hard.** Hard is in the Set Up menu. Use one weapon colour for the whole run and fire a shell whenever a capsule appears. Note where your shell stock runs out.
- **Run 2, the same difficulty with the opposite habit.** Hoard shells until bosses and change colour for each boss. Compare where you lost power.
- **Three two-minute Challenge runs back to back.** Cluster only. Learn which formations pay out when you shoot them together.
- **One five-minute run**, finishing on the last leader so the time and ship bonus counts.
- **Then the Special Version**, if you can find it, to see the Caravan stage as it was presented at the event.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** Soldier Blade suits interrupted sessions better than almost any shooter of its era. A Challenge run lasts two or five minutes, a continue restarts the stage, and the capsule system asks for a decision every few seconds without pausing the action.

**Friction.** The manual says high scores are saved only to a TurboBooster Plus. Whether a given emulator keeps scores between sessions wasn't checked, so write down any Caravan scores you care about. Some contemporary reviewers found the main game easy, and Uribo himself regretted cuts forced by time and memory: a scrapped asteroid stage, missile pods that were meant to fire homing shots, and big Bay Wolf ships that go down far too easily. If you already know the main game well, Hard and Caravan are where the challenge is now.

## Controls and emulation: what's known and what isn't

According to the manual, Button II fires, Button I fires Super Shells, Select changes speed, and Run pauses the Normal Game. That's a two-button pad plus Select and Run, so it fits any modern controller. **Nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** If you're mapping it, put I and II on comfortable face buttons and make sure Select is easy to reach mid-stage.

## How it landed

Contemporary coverage was warm, with a few complaints. *Electronic Gaming Monthly*'s October 1992 Review Crew scored it 7, 7, 7 and 6 out of 10. In the magazine's text, one reviewer called it "a solid shooter … high on action and fast-paced fun" despite little originality. Another dismissed it as an average shooter, apparently rushed out ahead of the TurboDuo. *GameFan*'s December 1992 Viewpoint was warmer. One reviewer called it "a great shooter" and "a must for TG 16 owners." The other praised the huge bosses and colour packed into a 4-megabit card. Wikipedia's citations give those GameFan scores as 80% and 88%. Wikipedia also reports that *Famitsu* rated it among the console's better shooters but less refined than Hudson's earlier ones. France's *Player One* found it too easy.

Later coverage, mostly of the 2007 Wii Virtual Console release, was more consistently positive. GameSpot's Frank Provo gave it 7.2 and praised its "friendly difficulty curve" and flexible power-ups. Wikipedia lists IGN's Mark Bozon at 7.5, Nintendo Life at 9/10 and Eurogamer at 7/10. Uribo is the one who's hardest on it: his 2012 recollection is mostly a list of regrets.
