## A boss rush dressed as a run-and-gun

Most action games pad their bosses with long levels. Alien Soldier does the reverse. Treasure's 1995 Mega Drive game has short stages full of weak enemies, which serve as breathers between a long series of serious boss fights. *Mean Machines Sega* put it plainly in July 1995: the traditional shoot-'em-up "has been turned on its head," because the bosses "take up the gameplay time" and minor enemies hardly matter.

That's why it's in this issue. You're in a boss fight within minutes of starting. Its depth comes from a small set of linked moves: a dash that makes you invulnerable, a counter that turns enemy fire into health, and a full-health super move that costs the health you've just earned. If you like Treasure's own *Gunstar Heroes*, modern action games whose bosses you learn through repetition, or simply hard games that don't waste your time, it's one of the purest examples of the form on any 16-bit console.

Sega published it in Japan on February 24, 1995. Europe got it in May, and North America only got it through the Sega Channel. Hideyuki Suganami led development. Wikipedia's development history says he originally wanted to build it alone, planned far more bosses than shipped, and finished it with help as the Mega Drive market shrank.

## Why the Japanese cartridge is the one to play

There are two cartridge releases, and they don't play the same. Sega Retro notes that the PAL release had its music adjusted for 50 Hz but not its gameplay speed, so the European version runs slower than intended. The Japanese cart runs at full speed. Its title screen also keeps the slogan that the European version removed: "VISUALSHOCK! SPEEDSHOCK! SOUNDSHOCK! NOW IS TIME TO THE 68000 HEART ON FIRE!", above the line "For Megadrivers Custom." As the capture below shows, the title menus are in English. Not every text screen was checked for this issue.

## The kit

These details come from an English-language edition of the Japanese manual hosted by gamesdatabase.org. It doesn't say who translated it. The manual calls the hero Epsilon 2.

- **Choose four of six weapons before you start, repeats allowed.** Each weapon has its own Force energy. It drains while you fire and slowly refills while the weapon is holstered. Pickups found during a stage replace the weapon you're holding. Picking up a weapon you already have adds Force to it.
- **Two firing modes.** Press A while crouched to switch between them. Fixed Shot plants you in place and lets you aim in eight directions, and it drains Force slowly. Free Shot lets you move while firing in eight directions, but drains Force quickly. Epsilon's body changes colour to show which mode you're in: white for Fixed, gold for Free.
- **Movement.** You can hover in mid-air, step-jump from a hover, walk on ceilings, and drop through or climb onto platforms.
- **Zero Teleport.** Hold down and press jump to dash at high speed. You're invulnerable for the duration.
- **Phoenix Teleport.** If you dash with full health, it becomes a flaming attack that damages everything in your path, at the cost of some health.
- **Counter Force.** Tap shoot twice to raise a shield that repels enemy shots. Shots it destroys become health items.
- **Eagle Eye.** Stop attacking for a moment, or use the Homing weapon, and the boss's weak point is shown.

The six weapons, as the manual rates them:

- **Buster:** weak, single stream, slow drain.
- **Ranger:** medium strength, fires in four directions.
- **Flame:** strong at close range, weak against mechanical enemies, slow drain.
- **Homing:** medium strength, tracks targets.
- **Sword:** strong, short straight beam, fast drain.
- **Lancer:** strong single shots, weak against biological enemies, fast drain.

## The loop under everything

Taken together, those rules form a cycle. You counter to refill your health, and once health is full, Phoenix Teleport turns it into damage. That drops your health again, so you go back to countering. The manual's own tips say this directly: destroy bullets with the counter, then "make full use of the Phoenix Teleport" when your life is maxed. Meanwhile you manage four Force meters, switching away from a weapon so it can recharge. You also choose between Fixed Shot, which saves ammo but leaves you exposed, and Free Shot, which keeps you mobile but drains fast. And you match weapon types to bosses, because Flame and Lancer have stated weaknesses.

There's a clock on top of all that. Every stage has a time limit, and the game warns you when less than 30 seconds remain. The manual also tells you to mash buttons to escape if you fall into a pit or get grabbed.

## Supereasy and Superhard

The game has two difficulty settings with honest names. **Supereasy** gives you passwords and continues, lets you adjust the game speed from the pause screen, and refills your health and weapon energy when you continue. According to Wikipedia, the enemies themselves are only slightly harder on **Superhard**. The real difference is that Superhard limits your continues, doesn't refill weapon energy when you continue, and is the setting that shows the final credits. Wikipedia and Eurogamer both give 25 stages. The boss count varies by source: Wikipedia says 26, Eurogamer says 31, and *Mean Machines* said "two dozen." Don't rely on any of them.

## A first-session plan

- **Start on Supereasy.** The aim is to learn the counter and the teleports, not to win.
- **Take Buster, Ranger, Homing and Lancer.** Homing helps Eagle Eye show weak points. Ranger handles crowds of small enemies. Buster drains slowly, so it's always available. Lancer is for mechanical bosses.
- **Practise in the first stage.** Before you move on, try ten Zero Teleports through gunfire, one Counter Force against something firing at you, and one deliberate switch between Fixed and Free Shot.
- **Get one Phoenix Teleport into the first real boss**, then counter your way back to full health.
- **Write down the password** at a stopping point, then do the same opening run on Superhard to see how much the continue limit changes your play.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** Each stage is short and ends in a clear test. You learn bosses by repetition, the way you'd learn the bosses of a modern roguelite, and Supereasy passwords let you stop at a gate and pick up later. The weapon setup screen means each run starts with a real decision.

**Friction.** The controls are the first obstacle. *Mean Machines*' playability panel noted that "initially the control system is a little troublesome," and that matches the manual's long list of inputs. The same review's overall summary says it "suffers from being a bit samey." Expect most of the variety to come from the bosses, not the stages. The manual and *Mean Machines* both say the HUD can be set up 26 different ways, including hiding it. If you change it, keep the per-weapon Force readout visible.

## Controls and emulation: what's known and what isn't

On the original three-button pad, A selects weapons and switches firing mode, B attacks, and C jumps. You don't need a six-button layout. **Nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** Practical advice for any controller or emulator: put B and C on face buttons that are comfortable to press together, because counter (double-tap B) and teleport (down plus C) are used constantly. Leave turbo off, since a double-tap is part of the moveset.

## How it landed

Contemporary reviews were good, but critics noticed the narrow focus. *Mean Machines Sega* issue 33 (July 1995, pages 80–81) gave it 85% overall, with 86 for playability and 87 for lastability. One reviewer called it "a real gamesplayer's game" and said you "get the feeling as soon as you start, that you are playing a classic." The second reviewer said that Alien Soldier "is saved on the strength of its two dozen bosses," and that it ends up with "more variety than other sprawling long-levelled blasters." The overall verdict was "a modern classic that turns the concept of platform blasting inside-out." Wikipedia, citing the original magazines, records *Famitsu* at 24/40, *Computer and Video Games* at 80/100 and Japan's *Sega Saturn Magazine* at 7.5/10. Sega Retro's review table shows reviews were widely spread, with scores in the 40s from some European magazines and the 90s from others.

Retrospective reviews of the 2007 Virtual Console release were warmer. Eurogamer's Dan Whitehead called it "manically-paced and often bizarrely creative" and gave it 8/10. Wikipedia also cites IGN's Lucas M. Thomas and Nintendo Life's Darren Calvert at 8/10, and quotes GameSpot's Frank Provo on the feeling, once the controls click, of being "an army of one" against huge bosses.
