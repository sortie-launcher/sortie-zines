## The brawler with no warm-up

God Hand doesn't take long to get going. 1UP's Andrew Fitch made that his main point in October 2006. There's almost no setup: you start in a dusty Western town, your companion points at some approaching thugs, and the fight begins. He compared it to the old arcade brawlers that "simply asked if you were a bad enough dude." He called it an old-school beat-'em-up, and argued that its depth comes from systems rather than story.

No filler and an instant start: that's the shortest fair description of God Hand, and this article explains why it's earned, and where the game's reputation for being rough is also earned. Clover Studio made it with Shinji Mikami directing and Atsushi Inaba producing. Capcom released it on PlayStation 2 in 2006, and it was Clover's last game. The details below follow the North American release (SLUS-21503); the localisation differences are listed under Controls.

## The controls, from the manual

The US manual lays out a control scheme that will feel unusual for a 3D action game:

- **Square** is the combo button. Pressing it repeatedly runs through the chain of moves you've built.
- **Triangle** and **X** are single attacks you assign yourself. X also throws items.
- **Circle** handles context actions: climbing ladders, picking things up, and special finishers.
- **R1** opens the Roulette Wheel. **R2** unleashes the God Hand.
- **L1** turns you 180 degrees. **L2** taunts.
- **The right analog stick is your only defence.** There's no block button. Eurogamer's Quintin Smith explains what each direction does: pull back for a back handspring, left or right to sidestep, both invulnerable for their duration. Push forward to weave in place, which only avoids attacks with short wind-ups.

The manual also lists a few special inputs: a low roundhouse kick (right stick up plus Triangle), a heel drop on a grounded opponent, a launch kick, and two follow-ups in the air.

## Build your own moveset

The manual's menu screenshot shows a Techniques count of 9 out of 114. You start with a few moves and collect the rest from scrolls dropped in stages and from the shop on the map screen, where you can also sell them. You decide which moves go into the Square combo chain and onto Triangle and X. 1UP pointed out that you can change your moveset at any point in the game, even during a boss fight. Some moves have special properties: in IGN's words, some juggle, some throw enemies away, and some break guards. Building your moveset means pairing slow, heavy hits that create space with fast ones that finish the job.

Two other resources sit on top of that:

- **The Roulette.** Press R1, tap R1 to cycle through the moves you've equipped, and press X to pick one. Each move costs Roulette Orbs: one for yellow, two for purple, three for red. Collecting Skull Cards raises how many orbs you can hold, according to Wikipedia.
- **The Tension Gauge.** It fills when you attack, taunt and dodge. When it turns orange, R2 releases the God Hand. For a short time you're invulnerable and faster, and you can really lay into enemies.

## 1, 2, 3, DIE

The system people remember is the difficulty level shown at the bottom-left of the screen. According to the manual, it rises as you deal damage and falls as you take it. Enemies get tougher as it rises, and stage-end bonuses get bigger. Smith adds that taunts, risky dodges and big combos also push it up, and that the levels are named 1, 2, 3 and DIE. Wikipedia adds two details that matter in play. At levels 1 and 2, enemies mostly won't attack unless you can see them or you're attacking them. At 3 and DIE they attack from off-screen too. A roulette move called Grovel lets you lower the level on purpose. Finishing the game unlocks a Hard mode that locks the meter at DIE.

So the game adjusts difficulty to your performance, and the adjustment is visible. Playing well makes enemies harder and pays more, and getting beaten up makes them easier. Smith described this as being unable to stay comfortable: even careful play eventually raises the level, until a beating brings it back down.

## Between the fights

The game splits each stage into checkpoints and saves between them. 1UP called this a design that lets you go "all-out" because a save point is usually close by. Between stages, a map screen lets you save, shop and visit a casino with slots, blackjack, poker and chihuahua races, as the manual and Wikipedia both describe. Smith describes random demon fights: sometimes, after you defeat an ordinary enemy, the sky goes dark, the music stops and a much tougher demon appears. Wikipedia says beating one always pays out money or a scroll.

## A first-session plan

- **Choose the lower difficulty** offered on the New Game screen. It's still a demanding game.
- **Before the first fight, open Techniques from the Start menu.** Put a fast jab at the start of the Square chain. Put a move that knocks enemies away on Triangle and a launcher on X. Then you have options for crowds and for single opponents.
- **Use the right stick constantly.** Sidestep anything you can see coming. Use forward weaves only against quick jabs.
- **Watch the level meter.** If you reach 3, back off and fight one enemy at a time.
- **Save Tension for groups of enemies.** Use R2 when you're surrounded, not when one enemy is left.
- **Stop at a map screen** and spend some money in the shop before you look at the casino.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** God Hand gives you a system you build yourself (the moveset), a difficulty that responds to you (the level meter) and resources you have to spend wisely (orbs and Tension). There's no long story setup between you and the first fight.

**Friction.** Its critics had a point. The camera stays fixed behind Gene. IGN's Chris Roper complained that enemies appear behind you and that dodges can send you backflipping into walls. 1UP agreed the game "could've really used a lock-on button." Smith found late-game difficulty exposed "cracks in the control system." The humour is broad 1980s action-anime slapstick, with groin kicks, spankings and a pair of camp gay stereotypes among the recurring enemies. If anime tropes put you off Japanese games, this one is full of them. The difference is that it never slows the action down to show them to you. Its stages are long arenas, so save at the map screens before you stop playing.

## Controls and emulation: what's known and what isn't

The documented emulation facts are short. PCSX2's upstream GameIndex rates the North American disc (SLUS-21503) as compatibility 5, which in PCSX2's scale means "Playable." It applies half-pixel-offset and native-scaling fixes so the post-processing effects look right at higher resolutions. That's desktop PCSX2 data; Android forks and other builds may differ, and **nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** Whatever you play on, map the right stick directly to evasion, not to a d-pad or face buttons, and keep L2 and R2 on separate triggers.

The North American release has a few differences, according to Wikipedia. "Chihuahua Curry" became "Puppy Pizza." The Japanese "Pan Drop" roulette move was removed. "Head Slicer" is included, which the Japanese version lacked.

## How it landed

Reviews were sharply split at release. IGN's Chris Roper gave it 3/10 on October 10, 2006. He called it "an extremely tedious brawler," and named its combat system, with its customisable combo strings, "one of the only interesting aspects of the game." 1UP's Andrew Fitch, writing a day earlier, gave it 8/10 and said it "does the best job at emulating the rush of old-school arcade action" of any game that generation. Eurogamer's Quintin Smith gave the 2007 PAL release 8/10 because it is "a fighting game with really good fighting." Wikipedia's review summary records *Edge* at 7/10, *Game Informer* 6, *EGM* 6.33, *Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine* 7 and *Famitsu* 26/40. OPM's review opened by calling it "a terrible, terrible game" that its reviewer couldn't stop playing. Metacritic put the aggregate at mixed or average.

Its reputation has improved since. Wikipedia notes that IGN later placed it at number 100 on its 2010 list of the top 100 PS2 games, that it appeared in *1001 Video Games You Must Play Before You Die*, and that it's now generally regarded as a cult classic.
