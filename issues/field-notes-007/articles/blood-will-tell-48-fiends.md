## A neighbour, not a pure stylist

Blood Will Tell sits beside the stylish-action family rather than inside it. Sega and Red Entertainment's 2004 PS2 game is usually filed as action-adventure. IGN's Ivan Sulic called it a string of pattern-based boss fights. It belongs in this issue for one idea the genre rarely tries: the hero gets more human as he gets stronger. Hyakkimaru starts with prosthetic limbs that hide blades and guns, and every fiend he kills returns a piece of the body it stole.

This article covers the North American disc (Redump serial SLUS-20782, English only). Japan got the game first, as Dororo, on 9 September 2004, and the US release followed on 21 September 2004.

## Tezuka's unfinished story

Osamu Tezuka's Dororo manga ran in Weekly Shōnen Sunday from August 1967 to July 1968. It was cancelled there, then concluded in Bōken Ō in 1969. The game keeps the premise: the warlord Kagemitsu Daigo trades his newborn son's body to 48 demons for power, and the doctor Jyukai rescues the infant and builds him a new body. Tezuka never finished the hunt for all 48 demons, so the developers wrote their own ending, as Brian S. Lang notes in Hardcore Gaming 101's retrospective. That ending is in an eighth chapter, which opens only after every hidden fiend is dead.

## What the fiends give back

With 48 parts to win back, it is tempting to assume each one changes how you play. Most don't touch your moveset or HUD. DEngel's GameFAQs guide reproduces the game's descriptions of all 48 parts, grouped into the twelve anatomy systems the manual mentions. Most are plain stat bumps. The heart, for example, adds attack, endurance and 56 maximum HP, and most of the seven chakras add luck.

The parts that do change play are what people remember:

- The left eye, won from the second fiend, turns the monochrome opening into full colour.
- The vocal cords, from the first fiend, give Hyakkimaru a voice.
- The left leg unlocks the dash, on L3.
- The ears let him land on his feet after being knocked into the air.
- The nose makes the controller rumble near a hidden fiend. The sense of pain makes it rumble when he's hit.
- A brain part lets the game keep battle records. Eurogamer's Tom Bramwell saw this as a kill count in the pause menu.
- Each recovered arm replaces an arm blade with a conventional sword. The right leg doubles the leg cannon's ammo and raises its damage.

Nor does the game let you target individual enemy limbs, despite a premise built around body parts. None of my sources (the manual, the reviews, the guide) describe it. The nearest thing is the giant fiend Mountainous, whose separate parts you fight in different areas. Sulic complained about the opposite problem: hits seem to land on large invisible boxes, not on arms and legs.

## Blades, guns and the Slice

L2 switches between Arm Blade mode, which is fast and acrobatic and levels each blade with use, and Sword mode, which uses whichever katana you have equipped. Hardcore Gaming 101 counts 30-plus swords, some with elemental resistances or occasional one-hit kills. Square is light attack, triangle heavy.

The manual confirms two guns. The right-arm machine gun fires on R1, only works in Arm Blade mode and holds up to 120 rounds. Bramwell disliked that it only fires while standing still. The leg cannon (reviewers say it's mounted in his knee) works by holding R2 and aiming with the stick. The manual gives its capacity as five shells; recovering the right leg later doubles it, according to DEngel's guide.

The Slice is the signature move. Charge and release triangle to paralyse a demon. Then key in a random X/circle/square sequence before time runs out and finish with triangle. More inputs mean better drops. Other enemies can still hit you during the sequence, and the move doesn't work on the 48 fiends. When the spirit gauge is full, square plus triangle triggers an invulnerable spirit attack. Against human enemies the game switches to non-lethal fights, where Hyakkimaru strikes with the blunt side of his sword.

## Dororo, co-op and the camera

Dororo, the child thief, follows you for most of the game and takes four orders: Search for items and hidden fiends, Attack, Collect, and Stand Close. If Dororo is killed, Dororo comes back but drops any items being carried. The manual confirms two-player co-op for most of Story Mode, with player two controlling Dororo. GameSpot's Justin Calvert warned that the camera ignores player two. Each chapter also has solo Dororo sections built on platforming and box puzzles, and failing one ends the game.

GameSpot, IGN and Eurogamer all criticised the camera. Calvert describes it switching without warning to a head-on fixed angle, which reverses your controls, and fixed angles that hide pits. L1 recentres the camera, and R3 toggles a boss camera that he says works far better.

## A practical first session

The first time you start Story Mode, it plays a tutorial and then the black-and-white prologue.

- Save at the first glowing save point. If you quit without saving, you lose the items and body parts you've gained since.
- Use the arm blades on everything; DEngel expects one to hit level 2 by the first village fight.
- Against Great Horn, the first fiend, switch on the boss camera (R3) and use both guns from range.
- Stop once the second fiend falls and colour comes back.
- Practise the Slice on ordinary demons. It is your main source of food and ammo.

## Fit and friction

This is the game's weak point. Calvert and Bramwell both call the start dull and repetitive, and Bramwell says the game won him over only after a week. If you abandon games that take hours to open up, expect to be tested here. GameSpot counted about 60 boss-style fights and put the seven main chapters at 15 to 20 hours. Lang took about 17 hours to finish everything.

Many fiends are hidden in earlier chapters. When you replay a chapter you've cleared, the map marks the fiends still left there. But the manual warns that fiend levels are measured against your weapons, and Bramwell warns that you can end up under-equipped and stuck in a frustrating loop. Calvert also complains about frequent loading. On the plus side, save points are plentiful, Eurogamer calls the restart system generous, and 100 of Jyukai's Medicine buys an extra life.

My judgement, not a sourced fact: this is a progression game, not God Hand. Play it for the body-part economy, and on a portable or in short evenings treat each fiend as one session.

## Emulation notes

Nothing here was playtested. PCSX2's upstream GameIndex.yaml (commit b3402c13, 21 October 2026) gives SLUS-20782 compatibility rating 5, which PCSX2's source defines as Playable. It also sets two hardware-renderer fixes, textureInsideRT 1 for "broken shuffles" and minimumBlendingLevel 2 for dark fonts. That is desktop data; other builds and hardware may differ. The game uses both stick clicks (dash and boss camera), so on a controller without clickable sticks, map L3 and R3 before you start.

## Reception in 2004

- GameSpot, Justin Calvert, 21 September 2004: 7.3. Praised the story and progression; criticised the camera, slow start and dated visuals.
- IGN, Ivan Sulic, 22 September 2004: 6.8. Criticised the camera, hit detection and "absurdly unnecessary" platforming; liked the bosses.
- GameSpy, Justin Leeper, 23 September 2004: 3.5/5. Liked the hero, the combo attack and the progression; disliked the Dororo stretches.
- Eurogamer, Tom Bramwell, 28 February 2005: 7/10.
- Famitsu gave the Japanese release 30/40 (as cited by Wikipedia). Metacritic's aggregate is 67 from 37 reviews.

I found no source documenting content differences between the Japanese and North American versions. What is documented: Japan had both an original release (SLPM-65526) and a Sega the Best reissue, and the European disc (SLES-52755) has five languages where the US disc has only English.
