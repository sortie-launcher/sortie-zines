## The 2001 hinge

Capcom's Onimusha: Warlords reached North America on March 14, 2001, months before Devil May Cry. Its body is a Resident Evil game: fixed camera angles over pre-rendered rooms, keys, herbs and an inventory screen. Its heart is a sword game that pays you for timing. Producer Keiji Inafune called the project "Sengoku Biohazard"; it began on the original PlayStation, built on Resident Evil 1.5, before moving to PS2.

![North American PS2 front cover of Onimusha: Warlords (NTSC U/C, ESRB M), showing Samanosuke, © Capcom, via libretro-thumbnails](media/onimusha-warlords-issen.webp)

It also touches Devil May Cry directly. In 2021 Hideki Kamiya said a bug in Onimusha, then in development on the same floor, sent enemies flying into the air when slashed. He went straight to programmer Kazunori Inoue and, in Siliconera's translation, said: "I want to hit enemies and see them fly into the air!" That request became Devil May Cry's air juggles.

## Resident Evil's skeleton

The US manual is blunt about the controls: the game "uses the directional buttons for control. The left analog stick is not used in this game." Up runs forward, left and right rotate, R2 turns 180 degrees, and L1 blocks. Holding R1 readies the sword, and Samanosuke can then step quickly in four directions. Square attacks the nearest enemy, down plus Square kicks, and holding Circle draws in souls. You save only at Magic Mirrors.

![The US manual's Game Controls spread: movement on the directional buttons, left stick marked "Not used", souls on Circle. Onimusha: Warlords NTSC-U manual, pages 6–7, © Capcom, scan via Video Game Museum](media/onimusha-warlords-issen-manual-controls.webp)

The structure is lighter than Resident Evil's. IGN's Doug Perry estimated 85 percent action, 15 percent puzzles, and GameSpot's Joe Fielder praised puzzles "built for speed". Twice the story hands control to Kaede, Samanosuke's ninja ally. She is faster, throws knives and cannot absorb souls; some collectibles can only be reached as her.

## Souls, orbs and the gauntlet

Every kill can release souls, which you pull into the Ogre's Gauntlet. The manual lists three colours. Red souls power enhancements, blue souls refill magic and yellow souls restore vitality. The note under that list is the important part: "The amount of the souls will vary depending on how your character defeats enemies."

Samanosuke finds three elemental weapons, each paired with an orb: Raizan (thunder), Enryuu (fire) and Shippuu (wind). At a mirror you spend red souls on the blade, the orb, or items such as herbs and arrows. Blade and orb level up separately, to level 3. Orb level also works as a key: elemental seals on doors need an orb of matching element and level, so upgrade choices decide where you can go. IGN's 2019 remaster review had everything maxed about 3.5 hours into a 5–6 hour run.

## Issen: the hidden layer

The manual's Actions pages never name Issen. If you press attack just as an enemy's blow is about to land, Samanosuke slips the strike and kills the enemy with one cut. Capcom's official remaster site sells this as "Deadly Counterattacks". A fan FAQ written by Goldsickle in 2004 goes further. According to that FAQ, an Issen kill drops two to three times the usual souls and nearly always includes yellow ones. A well-timed L1 parry sets up a "Hajiki Issen", and Issens can be chained from one enemy to the next. The FAQ also reports that locking on with R1 stops you from Issen-ing enemies you aren't locked onto, and that the only boss you can Issen is the second Marcellus.

This is the system that leads into the rest of the issue. Onimusha 2 made Issen its explicit answer to button-mashing. Authorial view: anyone who liked the God Hand deep dive in Field Notes No. 005 will find the same appeal here, a fight that gets shorter and pays better the more precisely you play.

## Dark Realm and Fluorite

After you beat the boss Stylado and collect the Evil Plate, a Gate Keeper offers entry to the Dark Realm. It is 20 sealed floors of enemy waves. After each floor you choose between a light that leads deeper and one that leads out. Leaving means starting over from floor 1 next time, but you keep what you picked up. Floor 20 holds the Bishamon Ocarina, which opens a bone door past the second Marcellus fight. Behind it is the Bishamon Sword, which has unlimited magic and kills any non-boss enemy in one swipe.

Twenty Fluorite stones are hidden across the game; the Vision Staff makes them visible. Fan guides list these rewards for finishing a run:

- 10 Fluorites: an extra costume for Samanosuke
- 20 Fluorites: the Oni Spirits minigame
- Clearing Oni Spirits: Ultimate Mode
- An S rank: an extra costume for Kaede

The Onimusha Wiki warns that five Fluorites inside the keep can no longer be collected once the Demon Gate appears.

## A first session

- Use a D-pad, not the stick: the original PS2 release ignores the left analog stick, so whatever controller you use, put movement on a digital pad.
- Hold R1 near enemies and practise four-way steps before you get greedy with Square.
- In the first enemy rooms, try to time Square to land just as an enemy strikes; count the yellow souls each Issen drops.
- Spend early red souls on Raizan and its orb, because seal levels gate progress.
- Save at every mirror. IGN's 2019 review notes that boss fights lack nearby save points and the cutscenes can't be skipped.
- Stop at the first save after Kaede's section ends; that gives a natural session length.

## Fit and friction

The game is short. HowLongToBeat lists about 4.5 hours for the main story, and Perry said a skilled player could finish in about five. It opens with a fight and has no tutorial, which suits players who want a game to get to the point. The friction is real: tank controls on a D-pad, no camera control, and enemies that attack from blind corners ("Fix the damn cameras!", Perry wrote). Cutscenes can't be skipped, and a death at a boss means walking back from the last mirror. Easy mode is hidden; fan guides say repeated deaths unlock it but disagree on the count.

This guide was researched, not playtested. For emulation, the upstream PCSX2 GameIndex lists SLUS-20018, which Redump confirms as the USA disc, at compatibility 5 ("Playable" on PCSX2's scale). It also sets partial texture preloading and a software-renderer FMV fix. That is desktop data; other builds and devices may differ.

## Editions and sequels

This article describes the US PS2 original. It offers English or Japanese voices; according to Wikipedia, the PAL release dropped the Japanese track. Genma Onimusha on Xbox (2002) is a harder remix. It adds green souls that enemies can steal to power up, new armours, an Ogre Tower and 30 Fluorites. The 2019 remaster has none of that extra content. It adds optional analog movement, widescreen with scrolling, Easy from the start and a re-recorded score.

Composer credit has its own footnote: the game credits Mamoru Samuragochi, who admitted in 2014 that his orchestrator Takashi Niigaki had ghostwritten the music. Of the PS2 sequels, Onimusha 2: Samurai's Destiny (US release August 2002) came from a different team. It added a gift-giving system for allies and kept the D-pad movement, which reviewers criticised. Onimusha 3: Demon Siege (April 2004) came from the original team. It switched to real-time 3D backgrounds and analog movement.

## Reception in 2001

GameSpot's Joe Fielder gave it 8.4. He criticised the length and the dub, and called it "the survival-horror game for people who don't like survival-horror games". IGN's Doug Perry gave 8.9, praising the upgrade system and score but marking it down for its cameras and length. The Los Angeles Times' Aaron Curtiss gave no number; his verdict box read "Bottom line: Sweet", with "repetitive action" as the drawback. Wikipedia, citing Famitsu, reports 35/40 in Japan. It was the first PS2 game to sell a million copies, and Capcom counts 2.1 million sold as of 2024. Later reassessment was cooler: IGN gave the 2019 remaster 6.5.
