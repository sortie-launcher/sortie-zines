## What "character action" means

Character action is a loose name for third-person melee games built around a deep moveset, enemies you can push around the air, and a scoring system that judges how you fought rather than only whether you survived. Its grammar comes from 2D fighting games: attacks that cancel into other attacks, launchers that knock an enemy upward for a juggle, dodges with invulnerable frames, meters that fill and get spent. Wikipedia's hack-and-slash survey lists knockdowns, meter management, cancelling, i-frames, hitstun, super armour and spacing as ideas the 3D games took from fighters.

Two tendencies recur, though not every game here has both, and neither is exclusive to the genre. One is grading. Devil May Cry rates your fighting on a letter scale that tops out at S, and Capcom's own page for the first game says that the more stylishly Dante kills, the more red orbs (the upgrade currency) he earns. Other games in this history have no running grade at all. The other is boss design that works like an exam. A boss behaves like a fighting-game opponent, with tells, punish windows and phases, and your moveset is what you studied for it.

Rock Paper Shotgun's 2019 explainer put the practical difference well. In a Batman Arkham game it is hard to make Batman look inept, but "a newbie to Devil May Cry is easy to spot." The character only looks as good as the player.

## Where the names came from

The Japanese term is the clearest. Capcom labels Devil May Cry "stylish action" (スタイリッシュアクション), and its current Japanese page for the first game says the sword-and-twin-pistol combo play "gave birth to a new genre called stylish action". In America, Devil May Cry 3 billed itself as "stylish crazy action", as GameSpot's review noted.

English has never settled on a name. Yahtzee Croshaw pushed "spectacle fighter" in Zero Punctuation. In his October 2009 review of Wet he called it "a term I'm still determined to strong-arm into common parlance", and it later became a Steam store tag. "Character action" is the other common label. I couldn't establish who coined it or when, and Rock Paper Shotgun dismisses it as "vague and uselessly ambiguous". This issue uses it because readers recognise it.

## Arcade roots: Shinobi, Ninja Gaiden, Strider

Sega's Shinobi (1987, System 16 arcade board) was a commercial hit, and its Mega Drive/Genesis sequels The Revenge of Shinobi (1989) and Shinobi III (1993) carried its sword-and-shuriken ninja action home. Tecmo's Ninja Gaiden, released in Japan as Ninja Ryūkenden, came out in 1988 as two different games: an arcade beat-'em-up and an NES action game released in Japan that December.

Strider feels closest to what came later. Capcom first showed it at London's ATEI trade show in January 1989 and released it in Japan that March. Its director, Kouichi Yotsui, told the Strider fan site LSCM in 2010 that the design grew from a question he jotted down on a hotel sofa: "How far could I take an action game with 1 lever and 2 buttons?" His answer was a ninja who sprints up slopes, climbs walls, hangs from ceilings and cuts with a plasma blade, the Cypher. Critics often trace a line from Strider to Devil May Cry. I found no Kamiya interview that draws that line himself, so treat it as a critics' family tree, not developer testimony.

The other root is the fighting-game combo. In a 2002 Edge interview, as summarised by Game Informer, Street Fighter II producer Noritaka Funamizu said he found combos as a glitch while playtesting: attacks timed tightly enough could chain before the opponent recovered. Capcom kept it. Cancelling one move into the next became the basic sentence structure of everything else in this article.

## 2001: a samurai, a bug and a devil hunter

Onimusha: Warlords came out in Japan on 25 January 2001 and in North America on 14 March. It looked like Resident Evil in feudal Japan, with fixed cameras, puzzles, and a samurai, Samanosuke Akechi, cutting through Nobunaga's demon allies with weapons you could upgrade. It became the first PlayStation 2 game to sell a million copies, and it accidentally kicked off a genre.

Hideki Kamiya was directing what was meant to be Resident Evil 4. On Devil May Cry's 20th anniversary in August 2021 he told the story on Twitter, as reported by Automaton. Producer Shinji Mikami kept suggesting they drop the Biohazard name, and finally said "this isn't Bio any more", forcing it to become its own game. Free of the brand, a programmer proposed floating enemies and fire-breathing phantoms, and Kamiya decided to make the enemies demons and "do whatever we want".

Then came the bug. Onimusha was being built by another department on the same floor, and in one build enemies bounced into the air every time Samanosuke cut them. Kamiya kept a straight face, hurried back to his own floor and told programmer Kazunori Inoue: "I want to cut enemies and knock them into the air!" Inoue made a stand-in upward slash by playing the downward slash animation in reverse. Combined with the gun auto-aim he was also building, that let Dante launch an enemy with his sword and fill it with bullets in midair. That move, the juggle, is still the genre's signature.

Devil May Cry came out in Japan on 23 August 2001 and in North America on 17 October. On 16 October, IGN's Doug Perry gave it 9.6, describing a hero who "juggles enemies up in the air with his sword and then switches to his guns in the blink of an eye". He still placed it in the Resident Evil family, noting its fixed camera angles were used "like Resident Evil and Onimusha before it".

## 2002–2006: ninjas, styles and Sparta

Sega's reply was Shinobi (2002, PS2), developed by Overworks, which took the series into 3D. Hotsuma's sword Akujiki feeds on souls and drains his own health when it goes hungry, which keeps you moving forward. The "Tate" system rewarded killing every enemy in a group quickly with a cinematic finishing pose and extra soul energy. In his review of 8 November 2002, GameSpy's Benjamin Turner praised the 60 fps movement but said targeting and camera problems kept breaking the flow. The sequel, Nightshade (Japan, December 2003; the West, 2004), starred the kunoichi Hibana.

Team Ninja's Ninja Gaiden (Xbox, 2004), Tomonobu Itagaki's reboot, made difficulty its identity, and Tecmo spent five years on it. On 26 February 2004, Greg Kasavin scored it 9.4 for GameSpot and called it "one of the best, most challenging action adventure games ever made". He noted enemies that don't follow obvious patterns and Ryu's Izuna Drop, which launches a foe, slashes it in the air and ends in a spinning piledriver. Kasavin credited the depth of the moveset to the studio's fighting-game experience on Dead or Alive.

Capcom's Devil May Cry 3 (2005) turned the moveset into a choice. You pick one of four styles: Trickster for evasion, Swordmaster, Gunslinger, or the defensive Royal Guard. Each style levels up with use. Kasavin's GameSpot review (8.6, 28 February 2005) called the opening unreasonably hard, partly because the North American default difficulty was the Japanese version's Hard mode. Our Devil May Cry 3 article in this issue goes deeper.

The Western branch began at Sony Santa Monica. In February 2004, David Jaffe told IGN he "loved Devil May Cry" and the Onimusha series, and that Ico inspired God of War's storytelling and puzzles. God of War (PS2, 22 March 2005) kept the combo grammar but made it broader and easier to read. The chained Blades of Chaos sweep crowds, quick-time events finish big enemies, and puzzles and platforming fill the gaps between fights. IGN's Ivan Sulic gave it 9.8 on 18 March 2005. Ready at Dawn later carried the series to PSP with Chains of Olympus (2008) and Ghost of Sparta (2010).

Kamiya's next step was Viewtiful Joe (GameCube, 2003, produced by Atsushi Inaba), a side-scrolling brawler whose VFX powers slow time down or speed it up. That team became Clover Studio, which also made Ōkami and, under Mikami's direction, God Hand (2006), covered in Field Notes No. 005.

## 2008 onward: Platinum, Nero and the heirs

Clover was dissolved, and key staff founded Seeds, which merged with ODD in October 2007 to form PlatinumGames. Kamiya's Bayonetta came out in Japan in October 2009 and in the West in January 2010. It made the last-moment dodge the centre of the game: dodge just before a hit and Witch Time slows the world down. IGN's Ryan Clements (22 December 2009) called that dodge "the most important technique" to master. Famitsu gave the Xbox 360 version 40/40, and Edge scored it 10. Both series grade at more than one scale. Devil May Cry shows a live style rank and also grades whole missions; IGN's DmC review notes that style points feed that mission ranking. Bayonetta splits chapters into fights called verses, grading each on time, combo damage and damage taken, then tallying a chapter rank. Our Bayonetta article covers it in depth.

Metal Gear Rising: Revengeance (February 2013) began at Kojima Productions, which struggled to make sword-based play work. PlatinumGames took over in 2011. Its Blade Mode lets Raiden slice enemies apart in slow motion and pull health items out of what he cut.

At Capcom, Hideaki Itsuno directed Devil May Cry 4 (2008, with new protagonist Nero) and supervised DmC: Devil May Cry (2013), Ninja Theory's reboot. He returned to direct Devil May Cry 5 (8 March 2019), which splits play between Nero, Dante and V, each with a distinct style.

The grammar has spread since. Tango Gameworks' Hi-Fi Rush (2023) put combat on the beat. Ninja Gaiden 4 (21 October 2025) was co-developed by Team Ninja and PlatinumGames, uniting the Ninja Gaiden line with the studio built from Kamiya's Clover team.

## What the genre asks of you

- **Learning before flow.** The fun arrives once the cancels and dodge timing become automatic. GameSpot warned that some players would "justifiably give up" in DMC3's opening missions.
- **Replays for grades.** Chapters are short and built to be replayed for a better rank, with harder difficulties as the real endgame.
- **Precise input.** Dodge windows are small, so input latency matters. Any extra delay from an emulator, display or wireless controller eats directly into those windows.

My judgement: anyone who enjoys short, dense action games that start fighting immediately already likes the core loop. The risk is the slow-learning openings of DMC3 and Ninja Gaiden, both famous for punishing their first hours. God of War is the gentlest entry, and arcade Strider is the most immediate.

## The lineage, game by game

These are the games this history leans on, with the editions that matter. None was played for this issue; the descriptions come from the sources below.

- **Strider** (Capcom CPS1 arcade, 1989) is the baseline. Sega's **Strider** for the Mega Drive/Genesis (1990) is the best-known home port.
- **The Revenge of Shinobi** (1989) and **Shinobi III: Return of the Ninja Master** (1993), both Mega Drive/Genesis, carry the arcade ninja home.
- **Strider 2** (PlayStation, 2000). The North American release shipped as two discs, the original Strider (SLUS-01142) and Strider 2 (SLUS-01163). A Strider 2 disc on its own holds only the sequel.
- **Onimusha: Warlords** (PS2, 2001), the Resident Evil-shaped bridge, followed by Onimusha 2 and 3. Covered in this issue.
- **Shinobi** (PS2, 2002) and its sequel **Nightshade** (PS2, 2003–04), Sega's 3D ninja games.
- **God of War** (PS2, 2005), with **Chains of Olympus** (2008) and **Ghost of Sparta** (2010) on PSP. Ghost of Sparta is covered in this issue.
- **Zone of the Enders: The 2nd Runner** (PS2, 2003), mecha hack-and-slash. Covered in this issue.
- **Blood Will Tell: Tezuka Osamu's Dororo** (PS2, 2004). Covered in this issue.
- **Devil May Cry 3: Dante's Awakening Special Edition** (PS2, 2006) and **Bayonetta** (Xbox 360/PS3, 2009–10). Both covered in this issue.
- **Devil Kings** (PS2, 2005), the Western version of Capcom's Sengoku Basara, with an added prologue.
- **God Hand** (PS2, 2006). See Field Notes No. 005.
- **Muramasa: The Demon Blade** (Wii, 2009), Vanillaware's 2D action RPG.

## Where to start

- **One credit of arcade Strider** for the 1989 baseline: movement first, sword second.
- **Shinobi III**, then **Strider 2**: the 2D form at its peak, then its 3D-backdrop revival.
- **Onimusha: Warlords**, the Resident Evil-shaped bridge, with the bug story in mind.
- **God of War**, the most readable 3D entry, then the PSP pair if it clicks.
- **Devil May Cry 3 Special Edition** once the basics feel natural; it is where the modern rules were set.
- **Nightshade** and **Blood Will Tell** for the side branches, then **God Hand** as the outlier.
- **Muramasa** last, a 2009 return to 2D.
