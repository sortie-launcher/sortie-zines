## Why this one belongs here

Koei's *Uncharted Waters: New Horizons* gives you a ship, a nationality and a real-world map of the 1520s, then mostly leaves you to it. There's a plot for each of its six captains, but it only moves when your career does. Your career moves when you make money, find coastlines, sink fleets or turn neutral ports into allies. Of the four games here, it's the one where the system and the setting are the same thing: the prices, the distances and the food supply *are* the adventure.

*New Horizons* is the sequel to Koei's first *Uncharted Waters*, and it came out on a lot of machines. Wikipedia says the game first appeared on FM Towns and X68000 in 1993, with the SNES, PC-98, Genesis and PC versions following in 1994. This article covers the **North American Genesis cartridge**, because its manual, its screenshots and its 1994 GamePro review are the main sources here. The box says *New Horizons*, and the cartridge label says *Uncharted Waters: New Horizons*. Sega Retro dates the US release to November 1994, after the Japanese Mega Drive version (*Daikoukai Jidai II*) in June 1994. The SNES version is the obvious alternative: Wikipedia credits it with better music, and it's the one that later reached the Wii and Wii U Virtual Console. It wasn't examined for this issue, so the screenshots, button notes and manual quotes below are Genesis-specific.

## Six careers, one economy

You start by choosing a captain, and the choice decides your career. Koei's manual splits them clearly: Joao, Ernst and Pietro are adventurers, Catalina and Otto are pirates, and Ali is a trader. Sega Retro lists each one's stats and special skill. Ernst is a Dutch cartographer, Pietro already knows celestial navigation, and Ali starts with Accounting, which lets a first mate tell you where a good sells best. Sebastian Sponsel's Sega-16 retrospective adds that the six stories cross over, so other playable captains can turn up in your crew.

Underneath all six is the same model:

- **Trade moves prices.** Sponsel notes that selling the same goods in the same city repeatedly drives the price down, so routes wear out and you have to change them.
- **Investment changes the map.** Put money into a port's market and shipyard and it develops. In the manual's words, you need to raise support to 75% or more to bring a port over to your country. Once it's an ally, new specialities can appear.
- **Diplomacy keeps score.** The manual warns that every allied port and every rival fleet you sink raises your standing at home and lowers it with your rivals. Push it too far negative and you can be refused entry to a country's ports. If you fall below zero with your own country, you can lose your rank or even your nationality.
- **Time is real.** A day has 24 hours, and every port building keeps its own business hours. The manual adds that you can't start a sea battle at night, and every battle ends at sunset, 9 p.m.

Combat is in two layers. Fleet battles are turn-based on a grid, with colour-coded ranges, and capturing a ship wins you its cargo. If your flagship meets theirs, the captains fight a **duel**. Sponsel describes it as a turn-based sword fight where you pick high and low attack and defence cards, and you have to save your strong options for later rounds.

## A first-session plan

The beginner tips in the back of the US manual are some of the best first-hour advice in any Koei manual. In short:

- **Don't leave Europe yet.** The manual is blunt: a new captain who sails beyond Europe is likely to wreck and drown. Even adventurers are told to earn a decent sum by trading first.
- **Make money close to home.** The manual's example is to trade port specialities on a short route, such as Athens–Istanbul. Use the Commodore's Cargo-Log of Goods to see where your cargo sells best. Buy ship supplies *before* you visit the market, so you keep enough gold and cargo space for food, water and lumber.
- **Let the game do repeat runs.** Once you have a route, use Auto Sail.
- **Upgrade for cargo, not cannons.** The manual recommends large-hold, small-crew ships such as the Xebec and Carrack. GamePro's 1994 tip was to buy big cargo capacity.
- **Hire a bookkeeper.** Sega-16 calls this highly recommended.
- **When you're ready for open water,** load more food than water. The manual says you can find springs on shore, and you'll want spare food for villagers. Follow the coast of Africa or the New World, and go ashore whenever you discover a village.

![What every early long voyage risks: "Commodore, we ran out of Food and Water. We must get supplies for the crew!" Genesis screenshot © Koei Tecmo (Koei), via Sega-16.](media/new-horizons-supplies.webp)

**Session goal:** choose Ali for the gentlest start, or Joao if you want an exploration plot. Run one profitable route in the Mediterranean until you can afford a better ship, then save.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** It's a slow sandbox built on tables of prices, distances and investments, the same family as Koei's historical strategy sims. There's no quest marker in the modern sense. The manual's own advice is to raise your fame in your chosen career and let the story follow.

**Friction.** This is the game in the issue most likely to test your patience, and it's better to know that going in.

- **It's long, and slow to click.** Sega-16's reviewer played it through twice before reviewing it, and warned that the controls take a while to feel natural and the game isn't for picking up and playing.
- **The interface is menus all the way down.** GamePro's 1994 review (more below) singled out the heavy button-pressing and icon menus.
- **There's only one save.** The manual says so directly: one game can be saved, saving overwrites it, and you should save often so you can carry on after "a fatal accident."
- **It's censored.** Sega Retro notes that the Western console versions swapped churches and mosques for "Round Earth Societies," turned pubs into cafés and removed references to alcohol. It's a historical setting with its edges sanded off.

## Controls and emulation

The Genesis manual's button table, reproduced below, is clear. In port: **C** selects commands and items and talks to people, **B** cancels and opens the Commodore's Commands, and **A** shows fame, gold and port data. At sea: **C** opens Sea Commands and the D-pad steers. Hold **A** and press left for fleet data, right for sea and provision data, or up/down for Quick Sail. **Start** opens Options (Save, Load, Map, Port, End Game), or the map when you're inside a building. Three face buttons map directly onto a modern pad.

![The button table from page 12 of the North American Genesis manual. © Koei Tecmo (Koei); scan via Sega Retro.](media/new-horizons-controls.webp)

On Genesis emulators such as Genesis Plus GX, battery saves from the cartridge should work like any other Genesis save, and a save state now and then is cheap insurance for that single slot. Don't mix the two without thinking, though: loading an old state can bring back an old version of the game's world. **None of this was play-tested for this issue.**

## How it was received

The best release-era source I found is the scorecard itself. GamePro issue 65 (December 1994) reviewed the Genesis cartridge in its Role-Player's Realm section, by its reviewer Bro' Buzz. The scores: Graphics 3.0, Sound 2.0, Control 3.0, FunFactor 4.0, Challenge "Intermediate." It noted battery backup and a $76.95 price. The review called the Genesis graphics and sound "rather flat," warned that the interface would put off some first-time Koei captains, and said an active imagination and a knack for numbers were the keys to enjoying it. It ended with a dry joke: you could read a history book or play this game, and "the excitement levels are about the same." Wikipedia sums that review up as calling the game an acquired taste.

Sega Retro's reception table collects more period scores, as percentages: Electronic Games (US, November 1994) 83, Beep! MegaDrive (Japan) 78, Weekly Famitsu 65, and Game Players (US, March 1995) a harsh 35. Its average is 73 across seven reviews. I didn't read those magazines myself. Treat those figures as Sega Retro's conversions, not a consensus. The later reassessment that's easiest to find is Sega-16's 2010 retrospective, which gave it 8/10: the long-term reward of a huge, varied world, for players willing to put in the time.
