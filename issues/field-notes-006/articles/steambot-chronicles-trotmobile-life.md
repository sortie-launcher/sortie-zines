## Why this one belongs here

Most mech games give you a garage so you can win the next fight. In *Steambot Chronicles*, you need the garage to make a living. Your Trotmobile, a two-legged walking machine, is your weapon, but it's also your delivery van, your bus, your fossil digger and the way you get to work. Irem built an open little country around it, with towns that keep hours, a band you can join, an arena, odd jobs, and a story that eventually lets you choose which side you're on. For anyone who likes both Armored Core-style garages and emergent sandboxes, it's an unusual case: a mech game where the customisation is mostly about your life.

This article follows the North American PS2 release, published by Atlus on May 23, 2006 (GameFAQs' release data, SLUS-21344). In Japan it's *Poncotsu Roman Daikatsugeki Bumpy Trot*. The title screen calls it "a relaxing nonlinear adventure," and GameSpot's Bethany Massimilla found something to that.

## The Trotmobile as a household budget

The US manual explains the machine better than most reviews do. A Trotmobile is built from **seven frames**: body, legs, left arm, right arm, grill, back and cab. The body and legs together set your carrying capacity. Everything else has to weigh less than that, or, in the manual's words, the Trotmobile "would just tip over." Heavier builds get more HP but burn more fuel. It's the classic weight trade-off from mech games, with a fuel cost attached that you'll feel all day.

The frames do much more than fight:

- **Arms** can be swords, shields or guns. GameSpot also mentions a pickaxe arm for prying fossils out of rock.
- **Backs** carry cargo and passengers. GameSpot describes fitting a flatbed for delivery jobs and running the machine as a bus.
- **Cabs** protect the rider from the weather, and **grills** start as plain headlights. The manual hints there are stronger weapons to find.
- **Garages** handle repair and refuelling, buying, selling and developing frames, swapping them, repainting, and changing your licence plate. GameSpot's one complaint here was that you can only change parts at a garage, not out in the field.

The manual says jobs need the right setup before you take them. Out on the road, the HUD tracks HP, fuel, a boost gauge and each arm's durability or ammo. Run out of fuel and you can't dash, so you crawl to the nearest mechanic. The manual also says that when HP hits zero "the game ends (and you blow up)." RPGFan's Patrick Gann, though, never saw a game-over screen: when he lost a fight, the story just carried on. Save as if the manual is right.

## A life with business hours

Off the machine, you're Vanilla, waking up on a beach with no memory. The game opens with a personality quiz, and from then on almost every conversation gives you several possible replies. GameSpot found that being consistently polite or rude changed the title people know you by, even when the plot didn't change. Gann compared the steady stream of choices to *Knights of the Old Republic*. He also said the first half follows a fairly fixed storyline. In the second half you have to decide whether to join or oppose the Bloody Mantis gang, and that choice changes the story missions and the ending. Side activities stay available either way. The manual's FAQ promises, with a wink, that it might even be possible to be a bad guy.

Time runs through morning, noon, evening and night. People keep routines, and the manual says only sleeping at an inn gets you from night to morning. Gann adds that shops and stations close at night. The manual warns that a starving Vanilla moves much more slowly, and Gann found he couldn't run at all while hungry. The manual's money list covers enemy loot, chests, busking, odd jobs, arena prizes and fossils. Gann adds billiards, dating, farming, a stock market and rhythm-game concerts with Connie's band, the Garland Globetrotters, whose five English vocal tracks RPGFan credits to Nadia Gifford.

## A first-session plan

- **Watch the tutorial from the title menu.** The manual describes Captain Ciboulette walking you through Trotmobile handling before the story starts.
- **Answer the opening quiz honestly**, then get to Nefroburg.
- **Find a parking cone and save.** The manual says you can *only* save by examining a parking cone, so make it a habit every time you pass one.
- **Visit a garage before your first fight.** Repair, refuel, and look at what your current frames weigh.
- **Take one paid job** that needs a frame swap, so you learn how a job, a frame change and a pay-out fit together.
- **Keep food in your inventory**, and treat the train as the fast way between towns when it's running.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** It's a mech game where the build is your income. Weight, fuel and how you earn money all depend on each other. Nobody is chasing you down a corridor: GameSpot described a "malleable story," and Gann compared it to *Shenmue*, only "a lot more lighthearted."

**Friction.** It's the wordiest game in this issue, and it asks for patience in places where mech fans may not expect to spend it.

- **It's slow to get around.** Gann called travel "slow and boring" and said loading is long and frequent. Holding the plot to a minimum, by his estimate, means about 15 hours of travel and fetch quests.
- **It looks and talks like an anime adventure.** The cast is cel-shaded and fully voiced, with plenty of chatter. If anime tropes put you off, this is the game in the issue that leans on them most.
- **The controls are tank controls.** Each analogue stick drives one leg, so the right stick can't move the camera. GameSpot found it awkward to take a thumb off the sticks for the D-pad to switch targets mid-fight.
- **The framerate drops.** GameSpot saw "noticeable graphical slowdown in a variety of areas" on a real PS2.

## Controls and emulation

From the manual's command list. **On foot:** left stick or D-pad to move, right stick for the camera, × to talk, examine and confirm, ○ for first-person view or cancel, △ for the menu, R3 to centre the camera, and Select for the Easy Map. **In the Trotmobile:** both sticks drive, the D-pad changes targets, □ locks on, L1 and R1 are left- and right-arm attacks, L2 dashes (push both sticks forward for a dash attack), and R2 jumps. L3 and R3, the stick clicks, pick up and throw with the left and right arm. The manual says the game is designed for the DualShock 2's analogue mode, so whatever you play on needs two working clickable sticks.

The PCSX2 wiki (October 2025 capture) describes Steambot as CPU-heavy and lists several open desktop issues:

- **A severe freeze** in the scene where Vanilla first meets Dandelion. The listed workaround is to set the Emotion Engine's Round Mode to Negative or Nearest.
- **Weather HUD loss**, where rain makes the HUD disappear in hardware mode.
- **A black ground** in the Sabbia Desert, with no known workaround.
- **Missing screen filters** in hardware mode.

An older black-screen bug in the shop menus is marked fixed. One PAL tester running PCSX2 1.7 still reported a black screen on their first garage frame swap. Android forks of PCSX2 and other builds differ in versions and settings, so read these as warnings, not predictions. **Nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** Keep several cone saves, and make one just before any big story scene.

## How it was received

Reviewers split between charmed and lukewarm. Bethany Massimilla's GameSpot review (May 24, 2006) gave it 7.1. She liked the variety and the lighthearted world, and criticised clumsy combat controls and slowdown. She also suggested it for anyone wanting to sink "upward of 25 hours" into "a wacky experience." Patrick Gann's RPGFan review (September 18, 2006) gave it 79. He called it "one of the better open-ended RPGs on the market," while saying he hated the travel. Metacritic puts it at 74 from 35 critics, with 15 positive, 19 mixed and one negative. Its normalised list runs from GameShark's 100 and 90s from GameSpy and the Official U.S. PlayStation Magazine down to 1UP's 80, which praised the game but mentioned its "janky controls." The PCSX2 wiki lists GameRankings at 75. In short, few critics disliked it, and most of their complaints were about how it plays, not what it's trying to be.
