## Why the garage starts here

FromSoftware's PlayStation 2 run of Armored Core stretched from *Armored Core 2* in 2000 to *Last Raven* in 2005. *Armored Core 3* is the hinge in that run, the point where the AC2 era ends and a new continuity begins. It rebooted the fiction, and *Silent Line* imports its saves, so the ACs, credits and emblems you build here can follow you into the sequel.

This article covers the North American release, SLUS-20435, developed by FromSoftware and published by Agetec in September 2002 (GameSpot and EGM give September 5). Japan had it that April, and Europe got it in May 2003. No source consulted documents regional cuts.

The setting is Layered, an underground city of stacked districts under artificial skies, run by a malfunctioning AI called The Controller. You are a Raven, a mercenary taking contracts from Mirage, Crest, Kisaragi and the rebel group Union through the broker Global Cortex. The plot is thin. GameZone called it "under-developed at best and totally unintelligible at worst." What matters is that every job is a priced contract, and your machine is the business.

## What a build actually costs

The Armored Core Wiki counts 230 parts from three manufacturers, none carried over from AC2. The key categories:

- **Legs** set your total weight limit, speed and turning, and your stationary energy drain. You can choose humanoid, reverse-joint, quadruped, tank or the new hover type. Hovers cross water, while tanks and hovers can't use boosters.
- **The core** sets how much your arms can carry and how many option slots you get. Over Boost cores give you a charged dash and built-in missile interception. Exceed Orbit cores give you autonomous gun pods instead.
- **The generator** sets energy output, maximum charge and its red zone. Empty the gauge and it locks red until it has fully recharged. One fan measurement put a mid-range generator at roughly thirteen seconds for a full recharge.
- **The radiator** sets your cooling. In AC3, overheating triggers "Output Down", which drains armour points until the heat falls. Nexus later changed that penalty to drain energy instead, so AC3's heat is a straight health cost.
- **Weapons** come in right-arm, left-arm, back, inside and extension slots. Left-arm guns are new here. Back cannons come with a posture rule: humanoid, reverse-joint and hover legs have to kneel to fire them, quadrupeds can fire them only on the ground, and tanks can move and fire.
- **Option parts** are passive upgrades that use up the core's slots.

The rule that makes AC3 a good place to learn concerns limits. In earlier games, an overweight frame or an energy shortfall stopped you deploying. In AC3 you can take one out anyway. An overweight AC slows in proportion to the excess, and a shortfall drains energy continuously. You can also purge parts mid-mission by holding all four shoulder buttons and pressing Triangle (right arm, back or inside part) or Circle (left arm). So a bad build costs you performance, not the sortie, and you find out why in the field.

## Contracts, consorts and the repair bill

Missions list an advance and a success fee. Some also pay per kill, and repair and ammunition costs come out of the payout automatically. GameSpot found repairs "can be quite steep", enough to discourage you from muscling through. Vesperas' GameFAQs guide calls missiles "a great way to run your money into a hole" and recommends energy weapons and the laser blade early on. Arena fights cost nothing to run. Corporate work branches: in Eurogamer's example, you defend a bridge for one company, then come back to plant charges on it for another.

Consorts are AC3's other new decision. Some contracts come with extra funds you can spend on an AI wingman: another Raven, MT pilots or air support. You can't give them orders. IGN noted that early on they "take too big a bite out of a mission's eventual payoff", though later they reward planning. Some hidden parts and top mission ranks require you to go solo, so the decision has real stakes.

The structure is 49 missions across eight districts, counting the Wiki's mission list (reviews round it to about fifty). Then there's a 72-pilot Arena ladder for money and parts, and a two-against-two Extra Arena after you reach the top. Difficulty is Normal or Hard, chosen once when you start a file. HowLongToBeat's user submissions average around eleven and a half hours for the main game.

## The first session

The first job is the Raven Test, an unpaid qualifier against eight weak MTs with Apple Boy as a free consort. No tutorial mode turned up in the sources consulted, so the first garage visit is the steep part, which matches what GameSpy and Eurogamer describe. Here's a practical plan, drawn from the Vesperas and DashXero GameFAQs walkthroughs:

- **Check your mail** after the Raven Test, as the FAQ authors advise.
- **Sell the starter legs and weapons, but keep the laser blade.** Vesperas swaps to reverse-joint legs and a pulse rifle, then takes two early contracts that paid him roughly 10,000 and 16,000 credits.
- **Turn on the cockpit read-outs**, especially remaining enemies, in the garage's display settings.
- **Save after every success.** Failed missions generally can't be retried without reloading. One FAQ also claims a career-ending failure-rate threshold that no other source repeats.
- **Leave optimisation for later.** DashXero suggests banking credits until you have 150,000 to 200,000, then upgrading in one go.

A realistic goal for the first sitting is a frame you chose for a reason, not the best one.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** AC3 makes your garage decisions pay out quickly. Missions are short (EGM: "reasonably short, but there are masses of them"), every one has a price, and you can see the consequences of a build in the payout screen. IGN found the difficulty curve gentler than AC2's, and an imperfect build still launches. For players new to the series, that makes it one of the friendlier PS2 entry points.

**Friction.** Three costs:

- **Menus.** GameSpy called the garage "improved-but-still-clunky", and GameSpot found buying and equipping "a bit cumbersome" because they are separate screens. Eurogamer complained of cryptic part codes.
- **Drab spaces.** Several reviewers found the underground levels murky and short on scale.
- **The controls,** covered below. They're the real onboarding cost.

It isn't instant-on in the way an arcade game is. GameSpot put the learning curve at "about 1 hour". GameSpy warned you "might feel overwhelmed" until you learn to balance power against weight and energy drain.

## Controls and emulation: what's documented

The stock layout puts movement and turning on the left stick or D-pad, strafing on L1/R1, and looking up and down on L2/R2. Boost is on X and Over Boost on R3. There's no right-stick aim, and Eurogamer reported you can't remap look to it. Every major review complained about this. IGN gave its gameplay score a 5.0, and GamePro blamed FromSoftware's "annoying distaste for dual analog control."

A community fix exists. VanLaser's free PS2 enhancement pack lists AC3 USA (SLUS-204.35) among its supported versions. It provides PCSX2 .pnach cheats or .ppf patches that move turning and looking to the right stick, using Nine Breaker's analog formulas. The author notes that in-game replays break. Not every PS2 emulator reads PCSX2 .pnach files, so check yours before choosing between the cheat and the patch. Field Notes did not play-test the stock game or the patch for this issue.

An archived PCSX2 wiki entry rates the US disc Playable but CPU-intensive, and testers there saw the frame rate drop as more ACs shared the screen. One AetherSX2 community report from a Snapdragon 865 phone marked it Playable at an estimated 30–35 fps. Those are community reports, not guarantees for any particular device.

## How it was received

US reviews in September and October 2002 called it good rather than great. Greg Kasavin gave it 7.5/10 at GameSpot, writing: "If anything is better than piloting a giant robot, it's actually building one." David Smith gave it 7.3/10 at IGN, calling it "unquestionably the best Armored Core game yet made" while complaining that FromSoftware kept "doing the same damn thing over and over." The others:

- **GameSpy:** Steve Steinberg, 82. He called it the most accessible entry yet.
- **EGM:** 7.5/10.
- **Game Revolution:** B+.
- **GameZone:** 8.0/10.
- **GamePro:** 3.5/5 for fun, calling it "just another step...and a rather small one at that."

Europe was harsher. Eurogamer's Martin Taylor gave the PAL release 6/10 in June 2003: "Accessible Armored Core 3 is not." Metacritic's aggregate is 74 from 23 critics. The main later coverage found is Heidi Kemps' 2025 PlayStation.Blog series history, a retrospective rather than a review, which credits AC3 with consorts, mid-mission weapon purging and the Arena's return.
