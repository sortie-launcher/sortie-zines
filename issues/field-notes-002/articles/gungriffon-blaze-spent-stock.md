## The counterweight

The other three games in this issue ask you to spend an evening in the garage before they pay off. *Gungriffon Blaze* is the exception. Its loadout screen has six slots, and three of them are decided for you. Its campaign is six timed missions. HowLongToBeat's handful of user reports puts the main run at a little over three hours. It's here as the issue's counterweight: a mech game whose tradeoffs are about what you carry into a fight and what you're willing to burn, not about building a machine from parts.

This article covers the North American release, SLUS-20080. Game Arts developed it, and Working Designs published it in October 2000 under its "Ultra Series" label: October 24 in most references, October 26 in Wikipedia. Capcom had released it in Japan that August, and Swing! Entertainment didn't bring it to Europe until July 2002. It was a launch-window PS2 game, arriving weeks after the console, and that shows in its scope.

Set your expectations by the documentation rather than the genre label. The US manual and the guides consulted for this article document limited ammunition, but not weight limits, heat management or orders to your squad. Your allies fight on their own. Expect a lean action game, not a simulation.

## The garage is a supply shelf

The US manual's Select System screen lists the slots: **body, main weapon, sub weapon, optional weapon, and two optional parts**. The main and sub weapons come fixed with the frame. You start in the HIGH-MACS III, which the manual describes as a lighter, faster design built for hit-and-run. By default it carries an assault cannon and flechette rockets. What you actually choose is one extra weapon and two parts.

Bakkufu's GameFAQs guide lists more than a dozen optional weapons: machine guns, a close-range shotgun, a gun that penetrates buildings, homing missiles, bouncing grenades, motion-sensor bombs, and a fuel-air explosive with a blast radius "well over 500 metres" that can't be reloaded mid-mission. The parts are firepower, reload speed, armour, a faster roller dash, a wheel adapter, a jump regulator, zoom, and an item-finder. You can usually stack two of the same part. The manual's example: two firepower parts double weapon power.

Then comes the catch. Gamezilla's 2001 review pointed out that whatever you equip "is taken from your reserve whether you finish the mission or not. Repeated failures can quickly leave you at a disadvantage." A Japanese-version quick guide on GameFAQs makes the same point: once you use an optional weapon, it's gone. Restocking happens through option boxes you pick up in the field. You only open them after the mission. The contents are random, and how many you get to open depends on difficulty and whether you succeeded.

That's the game's whole garage. Gungriffon doesn't price your build at the end of the mission. You decide before you launch which scarce item this attempt deserves. Saving the fuel-air bomb for a run you expect to finish is the kind of call Armored Core settles with money.

## Unlocks run on score, not success

Each mission ends on an Action Result screen. Your score is multiplied by difficulty and by whether you succeeded, then added to a cumulative total, and medals arrive at score thresholds. Bakkufu's guide makes the key point plainly: you don't need to complete a mission for its score to count. User-submitted unlock lists on GameFAQs give the milestones: the Type 9 frame at 13 medals, the Type 13 at 41, and the German Jagdpanther at six million points. Each mission also has a harder "Another" variant, with different time or weather and, according to the quick guide, fewer pickups.

There are four difficulty levels: Blaze Away, Normal, Nightmare and Hell. Harder settings multiply score, so they also unlock things faster. The GameFAQs list says the quickest route to the Type 13 is Hell mode. So it's a short game built for repeat runs, where a failed run still pays toward progress.

## The first session

Start on Guam. The manual describes it as training with no set objective or target score. The instructor, a Jagdpanther, waits on a hill for when you want to challenge him, and START ends the session. Guides suggest destroying everything there, including the plane in the south hangar, to learn the HUD before facing the instructor.

A practical plan, assembled from the manual, IGN, Eurogamer and the two GameFAQs guides:

- **Learn the enemy-direction bar.** There's no radar or map, just a linear indicator, a pickup detector and a compass. It's the most common complaint in the reviews below.
- **Respect your three jumps.** L1 launches a short glide, and the quick guide estimates 20 to 30 seconds to recharge each jump unless you fit the jump regulator.
- **Stop to snipe.** Zoom (Triangle) only works while stationary.
- **Unlock and take the shotgun.** IGN and Eurogamer both complained that the default homing missiles take forever to lock on, and Bakkufu rates the shotgun within 100 metres.
- **Use leftover time.** Failed runs still score, so shooting up the scenery before the clock runs out isn't wasted.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** Of everything in this issue, this is the closest to instant-on: no story between missions, short briefings, and timed sorties that, judging by the mission timers in review screenshots, seem to run about ten to fifteen minutes each. The allies fight competently on their own. GameSpot noted they don't "sit around and get their faces blown off 60 seconds into a battle." Eurogamer's Tom Bramwell noted the walkers "can cover ground at cheetah speeds," so it moves faster than "mil-sim" suggests.

**Friction.** It's short and thin. Eurogamer's verdict was "it's all over so soon," and EGM's Crispin Boyer said "the six here just ain't enough." Finding your next objective without a radar is a recurring complaint. GameSpot noted that Working Designs couldn't fix the slowdown. Pilot creation asks for blood type, IQ and eyesight, which guides report have no effect on play.

## Controls and emulation: what's documented

The manual's default puts aiming on the left stick and movement on the right, with R1 to fire, R2 to change weapon, L1 to jump, L2 to twist the torso and Triangle to zoom. Pad Config offers six presets and an inverted-aim option, and some reviewers describe the opposite stick layout, so check the presets first. VanLaser's free fan patch for this exact disc (SLUS-20080) swaps the left and right sticks. It's distributed as a PCSX2 .pnach cheat or a .ppf patch for the ISO; not every PS2 emulator reads .pnach files.

PCSX2's current game database lists the US disc with its top compatibility rating and no game-specific fixes. The only detailed test report, from 2012, mentions a sound squeal that may long since have been fixed. No reports were found for Android PS2 emulators. Field Notes did not play-test the game or the patch for this issue.

## How it was received

The US reviews from late 2000 into 2001 were good but kept running into the same complaint about length:

- **IGN:** David Smith, 8.4, October 25, 2000. He wrote that "Armored Core 2 may have depth to burn, but as far as actual gameplay goes, Gun Griffon is king."
- **GameSpot:** Frank Provo, 7.6. He found it "a fun, strategic game that doesn't ram reality down your throat," and noted that the US release used anti-aliasing better than the Japanese one.
- **Gaming Age:** Jason Allen, B+.
- **NextGen:** Eric Bratcher, 3/5. His advice: if you can only buy one mech game, AC2 gives more for the money, but this one "is well worth your time."
- **Gamezilla:** Jim Weber, 68/100. "Enjoyable for maybe a week."
- **EGM:** the December 2000 Review Crew's scores weren't legible in the scan consulted. Wikipedia lists 7, 6.5 and 7.
- **Eurogamer:** Tom Bramwell, 8/10, April 2001.

Metacritic's aggregate is 73 from 16 critics. The sources also disagree about the localisation. Working Designs' manual letter says it "rerecord[ed] some of the radio chatter," while IGN and GameSpot reported the English chatter carried over unchanged. No professional retrospective was found, and IGN's "Gun Griffon Retrospective" turns out to be a September 2000 preview.
