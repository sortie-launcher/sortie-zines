## Why this one, and why with caveats

Front Mission is a series that mostly stayed in Japan. The first game only reached English officially in 2007, as a DS port of its PlayStation remake, while *Gun Hazard* and *Front Mission 5* are known to English-speaking players through fan translations. *Front Mission 3* is where the series' official Western history begins. Square Electronic Arts' press release announced the "Popular Combat Strategy Series is Available for the First Time in the U.S." and gave a March 21, 2000 ship date. Other references put the date anywhere from February 29 to March 23. This article covers that North American disc, SLUS-01011.

It belongs in a garage issue because its workshop is a full design problem: weight against power, durability spread across parts, and a skill system hidden inside the hardware. It comes with a warning, though, because reviewers describe long stretches of story between fights. Hardcore Gaming 101's 2011 retrospective called it "arguably the most player-friendly entry in the series" and also a regression from its predecessor's strategic depth. Both halves of that verdict matter if you've played other entries.

## The wanzer as a budget

A wanzer has a body, two arms, legs and a backpack. Weapons go in the hands and on the shoulders. The US manual's rule is strict: you can't equip anything that exceeds the weight limit, and the limit is the body's power rating. Dave Connoy's GameFAQs parts tables show how far apart the extremes are. The Kasel M2 body offers 206 power, while the strongest bodies he lists, including a secret one, reach about 350. Legs trade weight for reach: Drake M2C legs weigh 18 and move two squares, while Kasel M2 legs weigh 59 and move six. Spare power isn't wasted, either. The manual says surplus raises "Fighting Power", the melee damage stat. That's why a lightly loaded wanzer with a fist can hit harder than its weapon list suggests.

Backpacks are the clearest trade. An item pack carries four, six or eight consumables, while a power pack adds 30, 60 or 90 power so you can carry heavier hardware. Connoy's prices show what's at stake. A part repair costs 100, a full rebuild of a broken part 400, and every item use costs 4 AP. One beginner FAQ recommends item packs unless you're short on power, on the grounds that repairs and reloads are your edge over the AI.

Then there's damage. Each of the four parts has its own HP. Lose an arm and you lose its weapon, and the shoulder weapon goes with it. Lose the legs and you crawl one square per turn. Lose the body and the unit is out. Your weapon choice decides how damage spreads. RPGamer's Jake Alley explained that rifles, missiles and melee land one heavy hit on a single part, machine guns and flamethrowers scatter hits, and shotguns split damage evenly across every part. A shotgun strips an enemy evenly, and a fist goes for the kill, so you're choosing how you want each fight to end.

## AP, counters and the hidden skills

Each pilot starts with 14 Action Points and regains 12 each turn. Moving costs 1 AP per square, attacks cost AP by weapon, and counterattacks also come out of what you have left. So the AP you don't spend on your own turn is your defence on the enemy's turn. Destroying enemy parts earns Ace Points, and enough of them raises a pilot's maximum AP.

Skills are FM3's real hidden garage. Every part carries one skill. If its conditions are met, it may trigger at random in battle and, once it does, the pilot learns it. Learned skills go into the pilot's computer, which has a limited number of slots, and they keep working after you've swapped the original part out. Registered skills can then chain into combos. RPGFan's Andrew DeMario described up to seven consecutive attacks against one enemy. Fan researcher Wazat counts 96 skills across the game's parts. The catch is that the game barely explains any of this. RPGamer's 2015 Retroview listed "Learning abilities requires a FAQ" among its cons. Budget for that, or for a paper list.

There's also the ejection system. Pilots can climb out, run to an empty machine and board it, helicopters included. Badly damaged enemies may surrender. Kill a pilot or force a surrender and you keep the wanzer's parts (but not its weapons) to sell or use. The IGN strategy guide's same-turn trick is to eject a pilot next to an abandoned enemy wanzer and climb straight in.

## The first session

In the first half hour, according to RPGFan's Patrick Gann, Kazuki's friend Ryogo asks him to come along on a job. Go with him and you get Emma's story, built around the US Navy. Decline and you get Alisa's, built around the Da Han Zhong (DHZ) side. The manual's flowchart warns there's no turning back. These are two complete campaigns with different allies and enemies, and some parts and websites appear in only one. Make that choice knowing it's a fork, not a dialogue option.

A practical first-session plan, drawn from the manual, Connoy's walkthrough and the IGN guide:

- **Turn on "Skip Battle Scene"** in Config. GameSpot noted FM3's attack scenes last only seconds, but every attack and counter has one.
- **Do the optional Wanzer Testing Site.** It's a proper tutorial, and its practice fights give free experience.
- **Battle Save at the start of every fight.** There's no save-anywhere. A mid-battle Battle Save takes four memory-card blocks, and a normal save between battles takes two.
- **Gang up on one target,** take the high ground for accuracy, and check counter accuracy with Triangle before committing.
- **Read the Network.** NPCs and e-mails hand out web addresses and passwords. The Network isn't required, but it hides parts and extra money.

Damage doesn't carry between missions. Connoy notes all parts are fully restored after each battle and fallen pilots come back, and there are no random battles. A bad fight hurts your medal ranking but not your next mission.

## Who it suits, and what it costs

**Fit.** Its pace has a natural unit: EGM's Che Chou measured 15- to 20-minute battles. A four-wanzer squad (FM3's sortie limit) against roughly eight enemies, with per-part damage, makes every fight a small engineering exercise. The capture-and-salvage loop also rewards building around whatever you manage to salvage, a habit roguelike players will recognise.

**Friction.** This is where it asks the most patience:

- **Talk.** Eurogamer reported stretches of "20-30 minutes of dialogue" between battles. RPGamer's second-opinion reviewer called the story "the visual definition of the term linear." The manual says START skips dialogue and movies, although RPGFan's Ken Chu found no way to skip the in-engine scenes in 2000.
- **Length.** Estimates run from GameSpot's "at least 50 hours" to Square's own "more than 100 hours" across both paths.
- **Translation.** GameSpot found the menus "needlessly cryptic". Eurogamer called the dialogue "B-movie grade", and Gann said in 2011 that the script "needs an overhaul".
- **Luck.** Hit locations are random unless a skill fires, and the Retroview's cons include "Luck sometimes rules."

If you came for the tactics, treat it as a game of battles and setup screens that you move briskly between, not a story to sit through.

## Controls and emulation: what's documented

This is a menu game, so a controller suits it. Per the US manual, Square opens the weapon list, Triangle shows counter accuracy, L1/R1 rotate the camera, L2/R2 cycle units and targets, and Select reveals units hidden behind buildings. Reviewers disagreed about the camera. GameSpot praised smooth rotation, while Chu described 45-degree steps and no tilt. DuckStation's own game database rates SLUS-01011 "NoIssues" with a small built-in display adjustment. That's the emulator project's rating, not a Field Notes play-test.

## How it was received

North American and European reviews in 2000 were warm. Sam Bishop gave it 8.8/10 at IGN on March 21, 2000, praising it as "how you organize an incredibly deep system into an easy-to-use interface." Matthew Mercer gave it 90 at GameSpy, calling it the finest strategy RPG he'd played even as he called the weight limit "a huge irritation." EGM's issue 129 gave it a Gold award, reserved for an average of 9.0 or higher. GameSpot's Ike Sato scored it 8.3/10, and Eurogamer's Rich Self gave the PAL release 8/10. RPGFan split, with Ken Chu at 78% and Andrew DeMario at 84%, and Game Revolution gave it a B−.

GameRankings' archived aggregate is 83.78% from 32 reviews. The later pieces are retrospectives, not release-era consensus: Hardcore Gaming 101 in 2011, RPGFan's 2011 PSN review (84%), and RPGamer's 2015 Retroview (3.5/5).
