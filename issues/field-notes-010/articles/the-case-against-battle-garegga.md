*Battle Garegga*'s rank system is a clever machine attached to a bad bargain. Once you understand it, the correct way to play is to play worse: fire less, leave power-ups on the ground, keep your spare lives low and spend them on purpose. The 1996 arcade game never tells you any of this. Field Notes 001 called Garegga "the one that argues back" and praised each credit as a lesson in restraint. This column disagrees with that issue. For most players, Garegga doesn't argue back. It bills you for the instincts every other shooter taught you, and it hides the invoice.

## The fire button is the trap

Everything the genre trains you to do raises Garegga's hidden rank. The Shmups Wiki lists the inputs: time, every shot fired, every item collected, every special weapon used, even every enemy bullet you destroy without a bomb. Only one thing lowers it, losing a life, and the drop is biggest when you have the fewest spares left. The community's own order of importance puts autofire first by a wide margin, ahead of medals, options and lives.

The autofire rule is the cruellest part. Tapping fire quickly raises the autofire rate. The wiki's technical breakdown shows that the per-frame rank increase follows the fastest rate you've reached in the credit, and dropping back to a slower rate doesn't undo it. So the most natural act in a shooter, hammering fire when the screen fills up, makes the game get harder faster for the rest of the run. The Japanese fan site VGF notes that the instruction card advertised the faster rates, and calls the result close to a swindle: from 12 shots a second upward, more firepower is a liability.

Read the wiki's rank advice as a list and it describes not playing a shooter. Stay at the default fire rate. Keep about two spare lives and die just before the next extend. Power up slowly, with fewer than four options. Skip pickups you don't need. Stop shooting when nothing is on screen. Damien McFerran summed it up in Push Square's review of the 2016 rerelease: play a perfect game and "you're going to be punished for your talent."

## Nobody was told

A tax is only fair if it's published. Garegga's wasn't. M2's own history of the game says that when magazines first printed its high scores, nobody had beaten it, and that players later pieced rank together through word of mouth at arcades. Japanese Wikipedia adds that the exact figures were never published and were only fully confirmed by analysis of the 2016 rerelease. Some arcades, by the same account, fitted variable-autofire devices and let players power-cycle the board to manage a system the game wouldn't explain. When operators have to install hardware to make a game legible, the game has a legibility problem.

This is the hidden dial at its worst. A setting you don't know exists isn't a choice; it's a trap on a delay. You collect an option in Stage 2 and pay for it in Stage 6, and nothing on screen connects the two.

## What its defenders get right

Field Notes 001 valued Garegga as an experiment you run one credit at a time, and VGF argues you can reach Stage 5 playing it like any other shooter, because your accidental deaths lower rank for you. Both points are true, and neither rescues the design. A lesson needs feedback, and Garegga's arrives stages late and unlabelled. The Stage 5 argument also concedes the case: the wiki calls Stages 6 and 7 "very difficult even at low rank and seemingly impenetrable at high rank." The ending is reserved for people who learned the rules somewhere other than the game. The depth is real; it belongs to whoever reads the wiki's hex tables, not to the person at the cabinet.

## The rerelease's confession

The strongest case against Garegga's rank comes from the people who love it most. M2's *Battle Garegga Rev.2016* added a real-time rank graph, which its feature page says reveals a system "shrouded in mystery until now," and an option to fix rank at a set level. M2 pitches its Super Easy mode at players who find rank management "so annoying" and feel that dying on purpose "doesn't mesh with my shmup style." Its promise to them: "Keep powering up, shooting, and destroying!" McFerran called the fixed-rank option a fix for one of the big issues the game's critics had raised.

That's the argument, written by the game's own preservers. The instincts M2 promises to give back are exactly the ones the arcade original punishes. Garegga handed players the difficulty dial and peeled the labels off. If you play it, play Rev.2016 with the graph switched on.
