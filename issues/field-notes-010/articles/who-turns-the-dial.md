Every game that adapts its difficulty to the player raises two questions: who gets to turn the dial, and can the player see where it points? Forty years of examples give the same answer. Adaptivity is the smaller idea. Visibility is the bigger one. A dial the player can read turns difficulty into information. A hidden dial is a promise the designer makes on the player's behalf, and that promise only holds if the dial only ever turns toward mercy. Hide mercy if you must. Never hide punishment.

## 1983: the first knob was a target

Namco's *Xevious* (1983) already watched its player. Hardcore Gaming 101's retrospective describes an arcade shooter that sends different enemy types once you get too good at killing one kind, and sends heavier waves as your score climbs. It also shipped with a release valve. Bombing a Zolbak, a harmless radar dish on the ground, makes the enemy force less aggressive for a while. Its designer, Masanobu Endo, said in 2014 that the goal "wasn't to program a super-difficult game" but one that new players could manage too.

Look at what that radar dish is. One of the earliest adaptive shooters hid a knob in the scenery and dressed it up as a target. A player who learned the rule could turn the game down on purpose. Nothing on screen said so. That tension runs through everything that follows: a dial the player could grab, if only someone had said it was there.

## 1986: Zanac puts it in the manual

Compile's *Zanac* (MSX, 1986; NES, 1987) did something new with the idea: it sold the dial as a feature. The North American NES manual gives it a heading, "Power Play Feature," and a name, ALC, short for Automatic Level of Difficulty Control. It says the strength and number of enemies change "according to the pattern of attack and player's skill level," and it lists some of the triggers. A high rapid-fire rate raises the level. Destroying a fortress lowers it, and failing to destroy one raises it. Programmer Takayuki Hirono, interviewed in 2015, added that enemies destroyed, enemies allowed to escape and shots fired all fed the level.

The MSX original went further and printed an ALC readout in its sidebar. Kurt Kalata, writing for Hardcore Gaming 101, notes that it's a hexadecimal number with no explanation, "just there to look cool." That is the first lesson in visibility. A number nobody can interpret isn't a window, it's decoration. Zanac named its dial and printed the reading, and still gave the player no way to read it. (This issue's Zanac article reads that manual page closely.)

## Hidden and kind

The big console games of the next decade went the other way: no name, no readout, help by stealth.

*Crash Bandicoot 2* (1997) is the cleanest case. Jason Rubin, Naughty Dog's co-founder, described the system in 2011 on co-founder Andy Gavin's blog. Die a lot and the game might give you an Aku Aku mask at the start of a round, slow a chasing boulder a little more each attempt, or turn a fruit crate into a checkpoint. The team's mantra was to "help weaker players without changing the game for the better players," and Rubin wrote that players "never realized" they were each playing a slightly different game. One correction to the folklore: the 1996 original didn't do this. Vicarious Visions, which rebuilt the trilogy in 2017, wrote that the dynamic difficulty adjustment was "originally only present in the second and third games," and added it to its remake of the first.

This is the hidden dial at its most defensible. It turns one way, only after you've failed, and it never takes away anything you earned. The promise Naughty Dog made on the player's behalf, that you won't be stuck here forever, is one almost any player would sign. Crash 2 earns its secrecy.

*Max Payne* (2001) made a different promise and didn't mention it either. Its default difficulty, Fugitive, is a range rather than a setting. Values pulled from the game's files by the Steam guide writer Mostly Harmless (community research) put the bottom of that range at enemies with 25% of their health against a Max with 200 health. The top end is enemies at 120% against a Max with 40. Your own durability swings fivefold, in both directions, and nothing on screen says which end you're near. A hidden dial that also turns up has stopped being kind. When the game gets harder, you can't tell whether you got better or the game decided you had.

*Mario Kart* hides its dial in plain sight. The *Mario Kart 64* manual describes the item window as working "like a slot machine" and promises an item that "might enable you to score a come-from-behind win." The slot machine is a costume. Super Mario Wiki's datamined tables for *Mario Kart 7* show the driver in first place drawing a banana, triple bananas or a green shell 80% of the time, while eighth place draws Bullet Bill 30% of the time and Lightning 25%. Hideki Konno, who directed the first two games, told Kotaku's Stephen Totilo in 2011, "We wanted to create a race where everyone was in it until the end." Satoru Iwata named the constraint in a 2011 Iwata Asks interview: the result can't "just depend on the luck of how the items appear," because the player has to be able to think, "It's a result of my driving."

Mario Kart is the one game here where the disguise is defensible. Its dial moves luck between people on the same couch, not between a player and the designer, and everyone can see the results land. The single item with an announced purpose is the Spiny Shell, which in the manual's words "will chase the Kart in the lead." It's also the item Totilo asked Konno to justify. Name the dial and someone will ask you about it.

## Resident Evil 4's open secret

Capcom's *Resident Evil 4* (2005) has the most sophisticated hidden dial of its era. The clearest public map of it came in 2016, when a speedrunner called Skullbot published an analysis worked out on a GameCube debug build. In that reading, rank runs from 1 to 10, starts at 5 on Normal, is capped at 6 on Easy and is pinned at 10 on Professional. It changes the damage you take and deal, the chance of critical headshots and even hitbox sizes. A missed handgun shot costs 5 points on a hidden progress bar, a knife hit costs 400 and a critical headshot earns 127. It also pulls toward the middle: the higher the rank, the faster it falls and the slower it rises.

That's a superb thermostat, and it fooled the best critics. Greg Kasavin's 9.6 review for GameSpot, published January 10, 2005, praised how the difficulty "scales up seemingly in perfect harmony with your growing comfort level." It did, because the game was measuring that comfort level. Ten years later, Engadget's headline still had to explain that the game had "secretly adjusted its difficulty for you."

RE4 is the strongest argument for hiding a dial, and it still loses. Its dial turns both ways. A player who improves gets a harder game and can't tell why. A player who struggles gets help and can't trust their own progress. The design is brilliant. The secrecy took something from the player: the knowledge of how well they were really doing. (This issue's RE4 article makes the full case.)

## The same director, one year later

This is the history's best twist. Shinji Mikami directed Resident Evil 4. In 2006 he directed *God Hand* for Clover Studio, and he put the dial on screen. Field Notes covered God Hand in issue 005, so here's the short version. A level gauge in the corner runs 1, 2, 3 and DIE. It rises as you deal damage and falls as you take it. A roulette move called Grovel lowers it on purpose, and higher levels pay bigger stage bonuses.

The idea is the same as RE4's: driven by performance, moving in both directions. The contract is the opposite. God Hand tells you where the dial points, lets you reach in and turn it down, pays you to leave it up, and gives its top setting a name that doubles as a threat. The adaptive system didn't get any smarter between 2005 and 2006. What changed was the player's relationship to it. It's the best design in this history, and it makes the case for this issue: the same director's adaptive system became a mechanic the moment the player could see it.

## The Director is a character, not a difficulty

Valve's *Left 4 Dead* (2008) named its dial and gave it a job title: the AI Director. Its Steam store page still says the Director "tailors the frequency and ferocity of the zombie attacks to your performance." Michael Booth, presenting the game's AI systems at the AIIDE conference in 2009, described something narrower. The Director estimates each survivor's "emotional intensity" from damage taken, incapacitation and nearby kills. It builds up threats until intensity peaks, then holds back for a relax period of 30 to 45 seconds. Boss encounters aren't affected at all. His slide is blunt: "Algorithm adjusts pacing, not difficulty."

So the most famous adaptive-difficulty system in games is, by its designer's account, mostly not one. It worked anyway, because players could feel it. A named Director turns invisible arithmetic into a presence you can talk about, blame and anticipate. This is the middle category this issue keeps returning to: hidden and named. You can't read its number, but you know it's there. (This issue's Left 4 Dead article goes deeper.)

## Grabbing it in the dark

Arcade rank sits at the other extreme. *Battle Garegga* (1996) and *Armed Police Batrider* (1998), covered in Field Notes issues 001 and 005, let skilled players grab the dial outright. In Garegga you hold your fire, skip items, keep only a few lives in stock and die on purpose just before an extra life, because dying is the only thing that lowers rank. The Shmups Wiki is frank about what that's like to learn: there "is rarely direct feedback if you are doing something 'wrong'."

That's the worst cell in the grid: a dial you can grab but can't see. The player gets full control and no gauge, and the only feedback is the late game turning into a wall. This issue's case against Garegga argues that letting players grab the dial made the game worse for most of them. I'd sharpen that. The fault wasn't the grabbing. It was grabbing blind. M2's *Battle Garegga Rev.2016* shows it. M2 added a Rank Graph gadget that, in its own words, "shines light on Battle Garegga's Rank system, which had been shrouded in mystery until now." The Shmups Wiki says it graphs rank from 0.0% to 100.0% in real time. It's the same game with the same rank, now with a gauge.

## The dial in your hand

The modern answer keeps the help and drops the secrecy. *Hades* (2020) has God Mode. Turn it on and Zagreus gets a Boon called Deus Ex Machina: 20% damage resistance that grows by 2% with each death, up to 80%. Greg Kasavin, now Supergiant's creative director, told Inverse in 2021 that the studio's focus was to "take the sting of failure and reduce that as much as possible, knowing that it inherently feels bad to die in a game." That is Crash 2's mercy after failure, rebuilt as a named, optional Boon the player switches on. It comes from the same writer who, sixteen years earlier, described RE4's hidden dial as "perfect harmony."

*Celeste* (2018) drops adaptivity altogether. Its Assist Mode lets you slow the game in 10% steps down to 50% speed, change the number of air dashes, turn on infinite stamina or invincibility, or skip chapters. Each assist is a labelled option, and the save file carries a badge while Assist Mode is on. The game's own text says Celeste "is intended to be a challenging and rewarding experience" and hopes the player "can still find that experience with Assist Mode." Nothing is guessed and nothing is secret. It's a dial with labels, handed over.

## The rule

Put the forty years on one grid and ask two questions: can you see the dial, and can you grab it? Adaptivity turns up in every cell, which is why it isn't the interesting variable. Visibility decides whether the same system comes across as a gift, a con, a trap or a mechanic.

So here's the rule this issue argues for. A designer may hide a dial that only turns toward mercy, because that's a promise most players would make for themselves. Crash 2 keeps it. Any dial that can turn against the player belongs on screen, as God Hand's does, as Garegga's finally does, as Hades' does. Hiding it doesn't make a game better balanced. It just makes the balance impossible to argue with, and players deserve to know what they're arguing with.
