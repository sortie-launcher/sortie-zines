Most difficulty systems are hidden because hiding them is the point. Zanac's was not. The US manual names the dial on page 4, lists some of what turns it, and seven pages later tells you how to keep it down. That matters more than the adaptive enemies themselves. Zanac's manual turned a secret into a rule, and a player can play against a rule. The best sentence in the booklet tells a shoot-'em-up player to shoot less.

## Page four

The NES booklet (FCI, code NES-ZA-USA) files the system under a sales banner, "POWER PLAY FEATURE", and opens: "ZANAC uses ALC(Automativ Level of Difficulty Control function)" [sic]. The typo is charming. The next boast is not: "This feature is unique to ZANAC." Takayuki Hirono, the game's lead developer, undercut that in a 2015 interview, saying the system was "probably influenced by Xevious", like every shooter of its day.

Then come the rules. A "High repid-fire rate" [sic] raises the difficulty. "Each appearance of an object in the air increases the enemy's strength." Destroying a fortress lowers it, and failing to destroy one raises it. The page ends by admitting the list is partial: "These are a few of the ways."

![Page 4 of the US NES manual, headed POWER PLAY FEATURE: the first line introduces "ALC (Automativ Level of Difficulty Control function)", followed by the rapid-fire, air-object and fortress rules. FCI manual NES-ZA-USA, undated; © FCI / Compile / Pony Canyon; scan via gamesdatabase.org.](media/reading-the-manual-zanac-page4.webp)

The page is a translation, and the original says more. Pony Canyon's 1986 Famicom Disk System manual gives the same three rules on its page 10. It also names a third variable, a "map level", next to the player and enemy levels. It cites a rapid-fire stick as an example of a high fire rate. And it warns that some enemies appear whatever the level. The English page drops all three. On page 3 it also tells you to "destroy ALC's", the enemy's central base. The Japanese manual calls that base Arukusu (page 7). Read literally, the NES booklet makes the dial and the final target one thing. It is probably a translator's slip, and it is the most honest line in the book.

## Shoot less

Printing a rule puts a price on an action. Page 11, under "Techniques For a ZANAC Victory", gives the price outright: "The more weapons your ZANAC launches, the higher the enemy's level increases. Avoid wasting weapons and hit the enemy with a single blow." Page 9 adds a release valve: shoot the Sart reconnaissance plane and "the enemy's level is lowered."

That is the publisher printing a guide to working the system, and players treated it that way. Wayne Yoshida and Zach Keene's 2003 community FAQ warns that with a faster main gun "you have to worry about the AI increase due to excessive shots fired." Hardcore Gaming 101 mentions "obscure strategies" for manipulating the AI and tricks for resetting it. A hidden dial is something done to you. Once it is printed it becomes part of your kit, like a special weapon whose ammunition is your own restraint. Battle Garegga would charge the same price a decade later without printing it (see The Case Against, this issue).

## What the page leaves out

The booklet lists what makes Zanac harder and almost nothing that eases it. Morihiro Shigihara's 2020 Morikatron article on the MSX original reports that the ALC falls sharply after your ship is destroyed. It also reports that equipping the Field Shutter barrier brings out strong enemies you never see with other weapons. Neither fact appears in the NES booklet or in the Famicom Disk System manual's AI section. The mercy rule did reach print in Compile's 2001 Zanac X Zanac manual, which says on page 36 that difficulty "drops sharply" when your ship is destroyed (Field Notes' translation).

The omission changes what a death means. Know only the penalties and every death is a failure. Know that the game eases off and it is still negotiating with you.

## A number is not a rule

The July 1986 MSX original went further than any manual: it kept the ALC on screen as a six-digit hexadecimal counter. Compile founder Masamitsu Niitani likened it to showing a level in an RPG, and said Hirono was "the type who wanted to show players everything he came up with" (Field Notes' translation). Kurt Kalata of Hardcore Gaming 101 called the readout context-free, "just there to look cool", and he is right. A number with no stated causes is a thermometer you can't act on. When the Famicom and NES versions dropped the sidebar, the counter went with it.

The manual's sentences outlived the readout, and they deserved to. Fire less, finish the fortress, shoot the Sart: that is a dial you can actually turn. In 2015 Hirono said a new Zanac should return to "a level of difficulty/rank that the player can clearly control through his actions." Pages 4 and 11 of a mistranslated booklet had already done that.
