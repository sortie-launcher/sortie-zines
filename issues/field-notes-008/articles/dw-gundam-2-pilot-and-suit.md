## Why it's here

Dynasty Warriors: Gundam 2 is this issue's mecha detour. Omega Force kept the Musou spine, one unit carving through hordes to take ground field by field, but swapped spears for beam sabres and thrusters. This article covers the US PS2 release (Redump lists it as SLUS-21873; released April 21, 2009). The game also shipped on PS3 and Xbox 360, and IGN called the PS2 port the worst of the three.

The interesting part is not the fighting but the web around it: rosters, licences, parts and relationships.

## Pilot and suit are separate choices

Wikipedia says pilot and machine are chosen independently, "allowing the ability to use any combination". The US manual is more precise. A suit becomes selectable only once you own every one of its parts; until then the Mobile Suit Lab shows "Not Enough Parts". Some suits also need a licence, earned in License Missions, and "some mobile suits can only be piloted by Newtype pilots." In Official Mode a pilot must clear all of their missions before other suits open up. Versus Mode is the exception: any suit with all its parts, no licence needed.

So: yes, eventually, behind gates. The two halves grow separately. The pilot carries a level (cap 50), Melee, Shot and Defense ratings and equipped skills, and the manual says the number of standard and charge attacks grows with pilot level. The suit carries its weapons, attack style and parts.

## Official Mode and Mission Mode

Official Mode is linear. Four Universal Century pilots, Amuro, Char, Kamille and Judau, replay arcs from Mobile Suit Gundam, Zeta, ZZ and Char's Counterattack. Mission Mode is the bigger game, an original crossover that, per the fan-run Koei Tecmo Wiki, adds pilots from G Gundam, Wing, Turn A, F91, Victory and SEED Destiny. The manual promises seven mission categories but names six: Story, Free, Collection, License, Friendship and Extra. The wiki's seventh, Download, existed only on PS3 and 360.

Most Mission Mode missions randomise allies, enemies, stage and time of day. Terminal messages sometimes ask you to choose; answers can shift relationships or open missions, and can't be revised.

Friendship is the most systemic layer. Each pilot keeps a separate rating with every other pilot, across seven tiers from Contempt to Friendship. Fighting alongside someone raises it; fighting against them lowers it. High ratings unlock Combination SP attacks with a nearby ally and special missions, and the wiki says License Missions often need affinity with the suit's original pilot. Who you fight beside today decides what you fly later.

## Parts are numbers, not a frame

Each cleared mission ends on a parts screen. You're always offered a part for the suit you flew and one for the suit of any enemy Ace you shot down, and downing many of one model improves its drop odds. Parts are shared by all pilots. Each (torso, arms, legs) has a rank and bonuses to Melee, Shot, Defense, Armor, Mobility or Thruster, and together they set the suit's SP attack level. Taking a part you already own replaces the old one, so every pick is compare-and-discard. In Mission Mode the Lab can attach special effects to a part, upgrade it, or develop it into another model's part; feeding it raises a Tech Level that opens slots and missions. Suits also teach skills, with the odds shown on screen, and a pilot equips three.

The honest Armored Core comparison: AC builds a machine from interchangeable heads, cores, arms, legs, boosters and weapons under weight and energy limits, and the result handles differently. Here a part belongs to one model. You complete and tune a Gundam's stat sheet; you never design a machine. The manual describes stats, SP level and skills, not new weapons or silhouettes, so my inference is that the loadout never changes. If design is what you want from a mecha game, this is a loot table with a spec sheet. If it's the loop of earning parts, getting stronger and redeploying, that loop is real.

## Fields and mobile armors

The map is a grid of fields. Defeating enemies drains a field's strength gauge; empty it and you take the field or sink its battleship, though some fields first send a Defense Commander. Victory conditions can change mid-mission, and Aces and Defending Officers pay Pilot Points.

Mobile armors are the new giant bosses. The English product description carried by ScreenScraper leads with the Psyco Gundam, which can "reconfigure" into a "titanic mobile fortress", and the fan wiki lists Dozle Zabi's MA-08 Big Zam. The manual's Smash Attack (hold Square) is, per the wiki, how you break their guard and expose weak points; destroying limbs ("Partial Destruction") cuts their attacks but yields fewer parts. GameSpot still found the bosses "large, predictable", and complained that an ally dying to one ends the mission however hard you draw its fire.

## A first session

- Play one Official Mode mission as Amuro on low difficulty to learn the HUD: thruster gauge, SP bars, field map.
- Press START for the briefing and check Player Settings > Action Info to see which combos your level allows.
- Practise Square-and-Triangle strings and the long-press Smash.
- On the results screen compare each part before taking it; duplicates overwrite.
- Then start Mission Mode, read the Terminal, play a Free mission and check Lounge > Relation Info.
- Need to stop mid-mission? Interim Save at the briefing keeps one slot, deleted once the mission is cleared and saved. Mission Mode also saves at the Lobby.

## Fit and friction

Solo is complete. The manual lets a second player join either story mode with START; GameSpot's PS2 review adds "assuming that the mission supports it", and IGN's PS2 review says split-screen worsened the stutter and blur. Versus offers War, Sudden Death and Hunting.

Failure is cheap, since Pilot Points survive a failed mission, and GameZone rated the difficulty Easy. The grind is both hook and cost: licences, part ranks, friendship, a story per pilot. IGN saw "a tremendous amount of content" for anyone who can "enjoy the repetition"; GameSpot found the fighting monotonous.

The story is the other wall: GameSpot said it demands "a deep familiarity with the Gundam universe"; IGN wanted a "Gundam Grand Scholar". For a newcomer, Official Mode compresses four series into short missions and Mission Mode is a cameo parade. The US release also dropped the Japanese voice track.

## The PS2 version

IGN's PS2 review found shorter draw distances, more stutter and an "overall blurry look", with content matching the HD versions; GameSpot said the PS2's draw distance makes space feel emptier still. Download Missions (fan wiki) and online play (IGN's PS3/360 review) belong to the HD versions; the PS2 manual describes only local Versus. Saves need 627 KB.

Manual defaults: L1 guard and strafe, R1 jump, R2 map, Square attack (hold for Smash), Triangle charge attack, Circle SP attack, X dash (twice to transform), right stick camera, R3 lock-on. Everything is remappable under Options.

Nothing here was playtested. Upstream PCSX2's desktop GameIndex rates SLUS-21873 compatibility 5, with no fixes listed; that is upstream data, not a tested result.

## Reception in 2009

PS2 reviews were harsh. IGN's Ryan Clements gave 4.9 and called it "a minor disaster". GameSpot's Kevin VanOrd gave 4.0: "not an aggressively awful game; it's just aggressively boring." GameZone's Dakota Grabowski gave 6.6 on a PS2-filed page whose text mentions achievement points, so his tested version is unclear. Metacritic averages the PS2 version at 52 from eight reviews; Wikipedia gives PS3 54 and 360 49. I found no substantial later critical reassessment of the PS2 version.
