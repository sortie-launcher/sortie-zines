## A war council before every fight

Dynasty Warriors 5: Empires came out in North America on March 28, 2006, for PS2 and Xbox 360. It drops Dynasty Warriors 5's character-by-character Musou story mode for Empire Mode: a seasonal map of China divided into 25 areas, a treasury, an officer roster, and one battle per turn. In most Musou games the battlefield is the whole puzzle. In Empires, last turn's paperwork is part of it too.

Despite the "Empires" label, and Wikipedia's "expansion", this is not a lock-on disc and it does not need Xtreme Legends. The US manual's setup instructions ask only for the Empires disc. If your memory card holds Dynasty Warriors 5 or Xtreme Legends data, the game offers to import it for some items, archive entries and Edit Mode officers, but only before you create an Empires save. PALGN's review called it a "standalone add-on pack". This article follows the US PS2 release.

## Seasons, gold and orders

Each turn is one season, four to a year. Turns open with events: rival rulers proposing alliances, wandering officers offering to serve, or earthquakes and plague draining gold and troops. A revenue report follows. Income arrives automatically and grows with your territory.

The real decisions happen in the Policy Phase. Your Orders per turn scale with the areas you hold, from one Order at a single area to five at sixteen or more, and every policy costs gold. There are three ways to spend an Order:

- **Consult**: one officer proposes two policies, and you accept both or neither.
- **Delegate**: an officer makes all of this turn's choices.
- **Assign**: you choose one policy, but only from those already unlocked.

That is the hook. New policies only appear when an officer suggests them, so Consult works as research as well as a decision. The eight policy groups run from commerce and smithing to recruitment, diplomacy and battle preparation: trade missions, bribing an enemy officer with an item, a three-year alliance, inciting a revolt.

There is also a moral axis. Rulers who stay fair unlock enlightened policies and lose access to cruel ones. Tyrants get the reverse. Happy peasants join your army when their homeland is invaded, and unhappy ones may join the invaders instead.

## Officers, borders and alliances

Officers are the resource that matters most. GameSpot counts up to three generals and three lieutenants in each area. You can only invade from areas next to the target, and only officers in or next to an area can defend it. Moving officers is free, but anyone left "Unplaced" is expelled. After a win you can hire captured officers for 400 to 4,000 gold, though GameSpot notes they don't always accept. A neutral starting area lets you build a court from unlocked officers or up to ten Edit Mode creations.

An alliance protects a border, but it also brings obligations. Allies ask you to join their attacks or defend their land. You don't gain territory from those battles, though the ally pays you in gold. GamesRadar's Greg Sewart notes that alliances can pull you into fights you would otherwise have skipped.

Two scenarios are open at the start: a historical opening, and A Gathering of Heroes, which scatters forces at random. Robert Dohner's GameFAQs guide says each historical win unlocks the next scenario, up to Flames over Chi Bi. Options include a 200-turn limit and permanent officer death.

## How the map reaches the battlefield

Strategy policies change the battle before it starts. You can begin with roughly half the bases, or with every base neutral except the main camps. You can add ten minutes or cut five, block enemy reinforcements, or hire sorcerers, bandits or juggernauts for one fight. You can stock five Tactics and ready two per battle, including setting the field on fire and a mode that halves your stats but captures any officer you beat. Items never drop on the field. Policies produce them, and battle consumes them.

You bring up to three generals and three lieutenants from areas connected to the target, and Dohner adds relief officers who arrive as reinforcements. Losses carry over, and only Military policies refill them. Bases are linked by supply lines: you have to push your blue line base by base to the enemy's main camp, or the camp won't fall. The D-pad gives every ally an order at once, and the pause menu can target a single officer.

## When it goes wrong

If you lose a defence, the area falls, and some of your officers may be captured and hired by the enemy. You can only fight one battle per turn, so when two of your areas are attacked in the same season, the one you don't lead is decided from its defences and troop strengths. A defender wins if time runs out. Lose every area and the campaign is over.

How often you can save mid-battle depends on difficulty. Interim Save is unlimited on Novice and Easy, allowed three times on Normal and once on Hard, and disabled on Chaos. You can always save at the Policy screen.

## A first session

- Choose the historical scenario and a modest ruler with readable borders; leave A Gathering of Heroes for later.
- Lean on Consult during the first year to unlock policies.
- Before invading, check the target's defences in Info against your officers' troop counts.
- Keep one strong general on each hostile border, and use a long alliance to cover the border you can't hold.
- In battle, follow the supply lines on the map, take bases in order, and press D-pad up to push allies toward the nearest base.
- Save at the Policy screen and stop after a defensive battle.

## Fit and friction

This is closer to a board game than to Romance of the Three Kingdoms. There are no province sliders, just a few Orders, a gold budget and bundled advice. The appeal for anyone who likes systemic strategy is that consequences carry forward: an under-garrisoned border, a broken alliance or resentful peasants show up later as battle conditions.

The friction is that every consequence is settled by the same kind of fight. GameSpot's Alex Navarro found the action as tedious as ever even while praising the strategy. Sewart describes 20-minute battles in which allied soldiers stand around, a lagging camera, and enemies who pop into view. One battle per season across 25 areas suggests a long campaign; that is an inference, as no session here was timed. A second player can join from the battle Information Screen, and Co-Optimus lists Empire and Free Mode as two-player split-screen. WorthPlaying notes that some support battles allow only one general.

## Controls and emulation

From the US manual: the left stick moves; Square attacks; Triangle performs a charge attack; holding Circle unleashes Musou; X jumps. L1 guards, and pressing Triangle just before a hit while guarding parries. R1 aims the bow, R2 toggles the map, R3 spends a Musou Token, and the D-pad issues orders. Nothing here was playtested. Upstream PCSX2's desktop GameIndex rates SLUS-21398 (NTSC-U) compatibility 5; that is upstream data, not a tested result.

## Reception in 2006

GameSpot's Navarro gave the PS2 version 7.2 and called the strategy "actually kind of fun". WorthPlaying's Agustin gave it 8.2 and recommended playing in co-op. GamePro's Johnny K. gave it 3.5/5 for Fun Factor, calling it "fairly competent" but light on new content. Wikipedia reports Famitsu's four 8s (32/40) for the PS2 version. Metacritic averages 62 from 23 PS2 reviews. This issue found no later professional reassessment.
