## The joke and the claim

Dynasty Warriors has a reputation that fits on a T-shirt: one hero, a thousand men, one button. Koei Tecmo's own pitch is not far off. Its Omega Force team page sells the Warriors games on "the thrill of 1 vs. 1,000". The reputation is not wrong, but it is incomplete. From the second game on, the better entries ask a different question: you can beat almost anyone, so where should you be?

That is the argument of this issue. In a good Musou game the kill counter is a by-product, and the minimap is the real interface. Gates, morale, bases, officers who flee or arrive late, an ally who will lose a fight if you are on the wrong side of the river: these turn the button-mashing into a string of routing decisions. Not every entry commits to that idea, and some barely use it. That gap is worth watching for as you read.

## It started as a fighting game

The first Dynasty Warriors (Japan, February 1997; North America, June 1997) was a one-on-one 3D fighting game for the original PlayStation. In a 2013 Polygon report on a Famitsu interview, programmer Akihiro Suzuki says the small team played Neo Geo fighters at lunch and talked Koei into buying a Tekken 2 arcade board "for research purposes". Designer Kenichi Ogasawara says Koei, then known for historical strategy games, had not yet made a full 3D game, and a fighter looked like the best way to learn. Omega Force was "five or six guys".

GameSpot's Joe Fielder liked it (7.2) because it was restrained. Combos stopped at three hits, all the fighting was done with weapons, and a well-timed parry left the opponent open. It is a curiosity now, but it explains the name. In Japan the game was *Sangoku Musou*; *musou* roughly means "unrivalled". The sequel was called *Shin Sangoku Musou*, which is why it became Dynasty Warriors 2 in the West even though it was a different kind of game.

## 2000: a battlefield in real time

Ogasawara also wrote the design document for the PlayStation 2 sequel. He told Famitsu, via Polygon, that the "one fighter with the strength of a thousand" idea was already there, but "Instead of just beating people up, though, I wanted that concept to be more about tactics, about having to come up with new strategies on the fly as you're fighting." The team began with enemy AI. Shin Sangoku Musou came out in Japan in August 2000 after slipping from March, and Dynasty Warriors 2 followed in North America in October.

Ike Sato's GameSpot review (7.8, written from the Japanese release) shows how much of the later series was already in place. Each stage opens on a map showing where your army and the enemy's are. Cutting enemy lines stops their reinforcements, and beating enemy generals lowers their morale. Sato also identified the problem that has followed the series ever since: "as your level increases, you can pretty much overpower enemies all by yourself," while allies often "just stand in one area". Wikipedia credits DW2 with the first Musou Mode, the story campaign, and Free Mode for replaying stages. Both modes still turn up in this issue's games.

## What the map asks of you

Dynasty Warriors 3 (Japan, September 2001) made the battlefield more readable. Stages include events that can raise your army's morale. With higher morale, allied soldiers attack more often, so where you fight changes how well your side fights without you. Gate captains guard the routes between areas and drop items. Two players could also share the battlefield on a split screen for the first time.

Brad Shoemaker's GameSpot review of Dynasty Warriors 4 (2003; 7.1) mostly complains that nothing changes. In passing it describes two important additions. Enemy officers can now challenge you to duels: a one-on-one fight away from the troops, a small return to the 1997 game. Siege engines also matter. Your army might push a battering ram toward a fortified gate, and you have to protect it until it breaks through. That is the basic Musou objective in miniature: the most important unit on the field is not you, and your job is to make sure it arrives.

Dynasty Warriors 5 (2005) kept DW4 Empires' stronghold system: bases you take by defeating their commander, which raises your side's morale. Alex Navarro's GameSpot review (6.8) counted that as a plus while complaining that Empires' strategy layer had gone. It is a fair summary of the series. Each new feature makes the map matter a little more, then levelling and stronger weapons let you ignore it again.

## Two kinds of expansion

The Japanese *Mōshōden* releases, sold in the West as Xtreme Legends, began with Dynasty Warriors 3 in 2002. Wikipedia describes the first one as adding an overhauled bodyguard system, new stages, weapons, difficulty settings and challenge modes. Later Xtreme Legends discs followed that pattern: more scenarios, characters, items and modes on the same battlefield rules. The DW5 edition in this issue is, in Eurogamer's words, "an expansion disc" that "also functions as a standalone game". Exactly which modes need the original disc is explained in the DW5 article.

Empires is a different idea. Bethany Massimilla's 2004 GameSpot preview of Dynasty Warriors 4: Empires describes a map of China split into regions. Officers make proposals: alliances, bribes to enemy officers, extra taxes, and resting troops, whose health does not otherwise recover between battles. Rival rulers offer trades. You then attack adjacent land or defend your own. In that version the battles stay standard Dynasty Warriors, while the campaign around them becomes a light Romance of the Three Kingdoms. Treat these as two separate design branches: Xtreme Legends widens the action game, Empires adds a strategic map around it.

## Samurai Warriors and the solo mission

Samurai Warriors (2004) moved the formula to Japan's Sengoku period. The structure is close to Dynasty Warriors, and GameSpy's Justin Leeper (3/5) said so. He still noticed two differences in how it uses space. Taking certain points on a battlefield cuts off the flow of new enemy troops, and some missions put you alone in a castle or dungeon on a time limit, where avoiding fights is the better move. Wikipedia credits the series with combat changes too, including a dodge roll and free combos during a Musou attack. Warriors Orochi, which appears later in this issue, combines both casts.

## Capcom's rival: Sengoku Basara

Sengoku Basara is often grouped with Musou, but it is not a Koei game. Capcom developed and published it, and it launched on PS2 in Japan in July 2005. Capcom's investor-relations history of the series says the design aimed to be "so easy to play even people without much gaming experience are able to mow down large numbers of enemies", with no blood, and with a cast of stylised warlords who drew a large female audience. Capcom says the "Sengoku Basara" games became a social phenomenon, a "female history buff fad" in Japan.

The Western version, Devil Kings, rewrote the story and characters as dark fantasy and, according to Wikipedia, changed the gameplay. Greg Mueller's GameSpot review (7.5) called it "pretty much" Dynasty Warriors with a fantasy setting. Its conquest mode has 15 territories, you choose battles next to territory you already hold, and it can be finished in about an hour. That is a quicker, lighter strategy map than Empires. Basara is a useful comparison because it shows how much of the Musou formula belongs to the genre and how much is Koei's.

## Borrowed worlds

Licensed versions became their own branch. Dynasty Warriors: Gundam (2007), Fist of the North Star: Ken's Rage (2010), One Piece: Pirate Warriors (2012) and Persona 5 Strikers (2020) used the battle loop for someone else's world. Damien McFerran's Nintendo Life review of Hyrule Warriors (Wii U, 2014) describes the job as controlling "as much of the map as possible by taking various castles by force". He also said the chaos often hides the tactical layer.

Fire Emblem Warriors (2017) may fit the formula best. Simon Parkin's Eurogamer review notes that Fire Emblem's weapon triangle makes you switch characters to suit the fight, that losing a character is permanent unless you turn the rule off, and that "at least as much of the game takes place on the minimap" as on the main screen. His complaint is a classic one for this genre: too much time in nested menus between battles.

## Origins and the question again

Dynasty Warriors: Origins (January 2025) is the clearest modern reset. Koei Tecmo's official site describes "a nameless hero" in the Three Kingdoms. Henry Viola's GamingTrend review (70/100) lists what changed: perfect dodges and parries, a troop-command ability, an overworld map between battles, and a story that runs only up to the Battle of Chi Bi. It also lists what was lost: a large playable roster and any co-op. Even here, a reviewer found that the tactical layer did not make "any noticeable impact".

Musou has never settled whether it is a crowd-clearing power fantasy or a tactical game in real time, and that is part of why it is worth playing. The five deep dives that follow each answer that question differently. In Dynasty Warriors 5 and Xtreme Legends, it is a choice between characters' campaigns and survival runs. In Empires, it is about which territory to hold. In Gundam 2, it is about pilots and suits. Warriors Orochi 2 is about three-character teams, and Strikeforce turns it into a PSP hunting party. Judge each one by how much the map asks of you, not by its kill count.
