## A Musou game that shrank the map

The rest of this issue is about reading a battlefield. Dynasty Warriors: Strikeforce throws most of that away. Koei released Omega Force's game in Japan in February 2009 as Shin Sangoku Musou: Multi Raid, and brought it to North America on April 28, 2009. It is a PSP game built around a small town, a noticeboard and missions you can finish on a bus. The officers and movesets come from Dynasty Warriors 6. The structure, as Destructoid, IGN and Pocket Gamer all said at the time, comes from Capcom's Monster Hunter.

For some players that will be the point. There is no morale bar to steer and no front line to hold. It is a loadout game: pick a job, pack a bag, hit things, bring home materials, change your build.

## Board, quest, loot, forge

You choose Wei, Shu or Wu, pick an officer and start in a village. The manual calls it the City. In it you take quests, buy and make gear, and switch between single-player and multiplayer play. The screenshot below shows Lou Sang Village, the same hub pictured in the manual.

![Lou Sang Village hub, a player named "AA" as Zhao Yun, English PSP text (region not determinable from pixels). © Koei/Koei Tecmo; screenshot via ScreenScraper](media/strikeforce-hunting-party-city.webp)

Quests come in two kinds. Story missions move each kingdom's chapters forward, and GameSpot found usually at least four open at once, playable in any order. Repeatable requests are there for levelling and farming. Leyviur's 2009 GameFAQs walkthrough shows how they're built. The first request, Bandit Trap, gives a goal (defeat the commander), a fail condition (die three times), and a bonus worth 50 gold if you open a gate within three minutes. Many maps are a chain of sections with sealed gates that open once you clear enough enemies. The manual says the revive count varies by quest, and running out the clock fails the quest. Pocket Gamer reported a 30-minute cap, with most missions taking under ten.

When a fight ends, your Deeds turn into experience and your materials go home: Long Shards, Bamboo, Obsidian and dozens more in Kracov's material list. Weapons, Orbs and Chi Skills all need gold plus the right materials, and drops are random. An Exchange trades common materials for rarer ones, but IGN and GameSpot both complained that its offers are random too.

## Fury and the third floor

Hitting enemies and taking damage both fill the Fury gauge under your life bar. With it full, Triangle plus Circle transforms you into a new model with spikes, glowing armour and sometimes wings. Press the pair again in Fury and you get a Musou attack, which ends the transformation. The Koei Wiki says Fury drains while active, and Leyviur's solo strategy relies on carrying items that keep it running.

The bigger change is going up. The manual lists normal and strong air attacks plus Down and Triangle to strike the ground from above, and Destructoid says R dashes through the air as well. GameSpot liked enemies spread over three tiers, forcing you to find alternate paths, but lost track of turrets firing from out of sight.

## The garage

This is the part for anyone who likes tinkering with a loadout. Every officer has a main weapon and a sub-weapon, swapped with Circle, and IGN noted that nearly anyone can equip any weapon type. When you lock on, the marker's colour shows how well your weapon matches the target, from blue (best) down to red. Leyviur's guide says sorcerers are weak to bows, heavy infantry to pikes and animals to tech weapons, and recommends carrying a bow alongside a melee weapon.

Orbs go into weapons and add effects like attack, defence or faster combos. Leyviur's guide says wood-element weapons poison, and calls poison the backbone of solo play. Chi Skills fill four slots, one on each hand and foot. They add things like double or triple jumps, extra dashes, regeneration and floating. According to GameSpot, upgrading a weapon raises either its damage or its maximum combo length.

The town gets upgraded too. Officers wander through and hand you Officer Cards. Pocket Gamer explained that you can slot six at once, and each one levels a particular facility after a completed mission. Since the blacksmith and storehouse compete for the same slots, you have to decide what to put off. You also take up to five items into a quest, so even packing involves a choice.

## A first session

- Go to the noticeboard; Leyviur's guide has the first request waiting there. Before the second, buy some Meat Buns.
- Lock on with L in every officer fight so you learn the matchup colours.
- Use the first requests to get a feel for Fury. In Leyviur's walkthrough the bar is full by the third officer of the second request.
- Come back with materials, make or upgrade one weapon, then pick a bow or melee sub-weapon to cover what your main weapon is weak against.
- Stop after the first story mission, Yellow Turban Menace. Leyviur expects you to have the materials and gold for a new weapon just before it, so you go in with your first real build decision already made.

## Party game, solo player

Here is where it gets difficult. The PSP version has local ad hoc play for up to four, plus a team versus mode, but no AI companions, as Destructoid and Pocket Gamer both noted. Leyviur called most missions "way, way too hard" solo; GameSpot called the solo difficulty "often overwhelming."

![Pre-release four-player co-op shot: Zhao Yun with players "MUSOU", "Leo" and "Gemini" against Diao Chan, Zhang Liao and the beast Bi Xie. PSP, English HUD, region not determinable. © Koei; GameSpot press gallery, March 2009](media/strikeforce-hunting-party-coop-boss.webp)

In a group, the manual adds a Multi Attack gauge that drops an item when two players hit the same enemy, plus friendship ratings that raise spoils, item trades and shared download quests. IGN saw slowdown with three or four players. Ad hoc play under emulation was not tested for this issue, so plan on playing alone unless you have real PSPs and friends: grinding levels ahead of bosses, rolling for materials, replaying requests. Editorial view: that only works if the build-tinkering appeals to you more than mission variety does.

## Controls and the PSP edition

On the PSP, the analog stick moves you and the D-pad controls the camera. Square attacks, Triangle is a strong attack (hold to charge), X jumps and Circle swaps weapons. L blocks, locks on and recovers you when knocked airborne; L with Up picks the main target, L with Down uses an item, and L with Left or Right cycles items. This comes from the English pages of the European PSP e-manual; we haven't checked a US manual. IGN noted you can't freely cycle lock-on targets, and GameSpot that Start opens a menu without pausing.

This article follows the US UMD release, ULUS-10416 in Redump, in English and French. The UMD offers an optional 300 MB install, which IGN strongly recommended; whether it matters under an emulator such as PPSSPP is untested. Koei's free download quests were a 2009 service, and we haven't checked whether they can still be obtained. Nothing here was playtested, including controls, performance or any camera remap to a second stick.

## Reception

The PSP reviews landed close together. Destructoid's James Stephanie Sterling gave it 7.5 on April 28, 2009. Ryan Clements at IGN gave 7.0 on May 5, praising the tougher combat and the co-op. Tracy Erickson at Pocket Gamer gave 3.5 out of 5 the same day. Brian Fishman's GameSpot PSP review gave 6.5; an archived copy dates it August 5, 2009, though the live page now says March 15, 2010.

Don't confuse these with the 2010 PS3 and Xbox 360 port. Sam Bishop's IGN console review on March 19, 2010, now shown as 8, describes AI-controlled teammates and online co-op; those belong to the port. We found no later critical reassessment of the PSP original.
