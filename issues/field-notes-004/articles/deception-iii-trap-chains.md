## Why this one: the stealth game played from the other side

The other three games in this issue put you in the intruder's shoes, reading guards and slipping past. *Deception III: Dark Delusion* flips that around. You're the one being invaded, you can't fight back with your hands, and your weapons are the building: a pendulum in the ceiling, an arrow slit in the wall, a spring in the floor, and the timing to make them hit one after another.

It sits between the first *Deception* (*Tecmo's Deception: Invitation to Darkness*, first released in Japan in 1996), where you also built the castle, and *Trapt* on PS2. Tecmo released it in Japan on 9 December 1999 as 蒼魔灯 *Sōmatō*, and in North America around 1 March 2000 (Wikipedia says 29 February). This issue found no confirmed PAL release: Hardcore Gaming 101 mentions one in the UK, but Redump, PSX Data Center and GameFAQs record no European disc. For an English-language copy, look for the US disc, SLUS-01067. The US version keeps Japanese voices with English subtitles in its FMV.

A naming trap if you're searching for it: the Japanese title *Kagero II: Dark Illusion* belongs to *Trapt*, not to this game. The game between the first *Deception* and this one is *Kagero: Deception II* (1998), which introduced chained traps.

## The loop, in plain terms

Reina, a 17-year-old orphan, is kidnapped with her family and shipped to the island of Alendar to be sold as a slave. A young man named Marco frees her in the prison and tells her to defend herself with traps. From there, chapter after chapter, people come to kill, capture or rob her, and she makes them regret it. (The US back cover calls her "Layna"; the game says Reina, and lets you rename her.)

Every chapter follows the same pattern: intermission, story lead-in, trap battle, story ending, evaluation. The trap battle is the game. The US manual lays out the rules:

- There are three trap slots, each on its own button: **ceiling** (Triangle), **wall** (Square) and **floor** (X).
- You bring up to nine traps into a mission, no more than three of each type, and you can set one of each type per room.
- Traps can't go in hallways, doorways or on squares with room devices, and a ceiling and floor trap can't share a square.
- Each trap needs to charge after you set it, and again after every use.
- Pressing Circle opens **Camp Mode**, an overhead grid where you can place or swap traps in real time while the intruder is still hunting you.

Rooms also come with **devices**. Some work with a switch; others are set off when a trap knocks someone into them: pillars, barrels, caskets, an iron maiden. Rocks roll down stairs and electricity spreads through water. Unlike the first *Deception*, you don't build the castle. The maps are fixed; what changes is how you wire them.

## The combo engine

What makes this game, and its predecessor *Kagero: Deception II*, is **chaining**. Hit an intruder again before his red damage gauge recovers and the hits combine, up to 15. The manual's expert tip is to swap traps in Camp Mode mid-combo, because three slots alone stop at three hits. Its example: a Spring Floor throws the victim up, a Float Magnet catches him, you set a Blast Bomb while he hangs, a Pendulum knocks him onto it, and the bomb goes off.

Each hit produces **Ark**, a "flame of the soul". At the end of the mission, your Ark is converted to **Dreak**, the currency for building traps.

Trap-making is where *Deception III* adds something of its own. You build traps from four parts:

- A **Base Circle** sets the trap type, such as Pendulum, Arrow Slit or Spring Floor.
- An **Emblem** adds a property such as fire, thunder or impact.
- A **Ring** changes how it activates. The manual lists Charge (charges faster), Timer, Summon (pulls enemies within four squares toward it), Auto and Damage (hits harder), plus a sixth, secret ring.
- **Power Orbs** raise its level.

So "A. Toll Hammer Lv.2" is a Pendulum with a Thunder Emblem, an Auto Ring and two Orbs. "Make New" costs more but keeps the original trap. Modifying an existing trap costs less but uses it up. GameSpot also warned that breaking a trap down doesn't refund what you paid for it.

The intruders push back. The Intruder Data screen shows each visitor's health, defence, speed, attacks and **Attention**, his ability to spot traps. The manual also warns of a hidden **Trap Guard** not shown there: some intruders block bombs or jump clear of floor traps, but only while unhurt. A fan FAQ lists enemies who dive through doorways, leap down stairs, put out the lights, warp or run faster when angry, and some are immune to fire, electricity or magnets. Reading that screen is this game's version of reading a patrol route.

## The first session

Chapter I takes place in Hades Jail against Gordon the torturer, who doesn't move, and the soldier Jade, who hunts you: one stationary target and one moving one. Chapter II, in a factory, adds villagers and the advice to lure them into room devices. *Official US PlayStation Magazine* called the early chapters "very friendly to newcomers".

Outside the story there's a **Trap License** mode (seven grades of five lessons, each ending in an exam), an **Expert** mode of 100 challenge missions, and **Free Training**, which replays any story map with a dummy you can position. For short sessions, Free Training is the best mode on the disc: a sandbox for testing combos without the story.

The story has four endings, and Hardcore Gaming 101 says none of them is happy. A choice in Chapter XIV splits the story: one branch runs 22 chapters, the other 24.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** This is a systems game disguised as a horror game. The fun is engineering: read the intruder's stats, choose a room, set three traps and a device, then try to get a long chain out of a combination you've never tried before. Players who enjoy designing kill rooms in a colony sim more than fighting in them will recognise the appeal. In the series, it bridges the first *Deception*'s castle-building and *Trapt*'s room traps, which arrived more than five years later on PS2.

**Friction.** The release-era reviews were blunt:

- **Repetition.** GameSpot's Joe Fielder called it more like a new season of a sports game than a full sequel, and IGN's David Zdyrko found it repetitive. GamePro said it wears thin after about a dozen missions.
- **Translation.** EGM's reviewers called it "appalling", and one reviewer wrote that moments meant to be tense end up funny.
- **Performance.** GameSpot and GamePro both noted framerate drops on the reused *Kagero* engine.
- **Tone.** OPM pointed out that most of your victims deserve it, which blunts the game's edge, if that matters to you.

There's no difficulty setting, and I found no sourced figure for chapter length. Chapters are self-contained, and you save during intermissions.

## Controls and emulation: what's known, what isn't

The manual says both analog sticks are disabled. You move tank-style on the D-pad. Double-tapping L1 or R1 rolls sideways, L1+R1 looks behind you, and R2 snaps round to face the intruder. GameFan praised the quick turn and roll as improvements over *Kagero*; GamePro said the lack of analog makes running away harder. DualShock vibration works.

DuckStation's game database lists the US, Japanese and Japanese budget discs as digital-controller games with no special fixes. Redump records no LibCrypt protection on the US or Japanese discs. **Nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** If you're setting it up, play Chapter I with the D-pad and try Camp Mode once before settling in.

## A first-session plan

- **Do the first Trap License grade before Chapter I.** It teaches charge times and placement rules faster than the story does.
- **In Chapter I, chain a floor trap into a wall trap.** That's the whole game in two hits.
- **Read Intruder Data every chapter.** High Attention means a trap he can see coming. Lead with something he won't expect.
- **Spend Dreak on a Charge Ring early.** Faster recharge means more combos.
- **Use Free Training to test a long chain** before trying it in the story.

## How it was received

Reviews in 2000 split between people who loved the trap engine and people who had seen it before:

- **GameSpot**, Joe Fielder, 29 February 2000: 6/10.
- **IGN**, David Zdyrko, March 2000: 6. It "definitely isn't a bad game, but it's definitely not a really good game either."
- **Official US PlayStation Magazine**, Wataru Maruyama, April 2000: 4/5. He praised the branching and replay value.
- **Next Generation** #64, Eric Bratcher, April 2000: 3/5, calling it "a creative niche title".
- **GameFan**, George Ngo, April 2000: 97, calling it a refinement of *Kagero* that fixed that game's annoyances.
- **Game Informer**, May 2000: 6.25. **GameRevolution**: B.
- **Famitsu**, for the Japanese release: 31/40.

EGM's individual scores are only available via Wikipedia. GameRankings' normalised average is 71 percent from 18 reviews. Hardcore Gaming 101, in a 2009 retrospective, called it one of the stronger games in the series.
