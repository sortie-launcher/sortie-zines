## Why this one: a ninja who works for everybody

Tenchu asks you to serve one lord well. Shinobido asks a nastier question: what happens when a ninja works for all three sides of a war, and the war keeps score?

Acquire made the first two Tenchu games, then built this new series with Spike, which published it in Japan on 10 November 2005 as *Shinobido: Imashime*. Sony Computer Entertainment Europe released it on 30 June 2006 as *Shinobido: Way of the Ninja*, on a five-language PAL disc (SCES-53931). There was never a North American release, so for English speakers the European disc is the edition to look for.

The PAL version is the friendlier one. It adds an English dub and English subtitles but keeps the Japanese voices as an option; according to Japanese sources the original had no subtitles at all. A detailed fan FAQ also lists small PAL-only changes: the stealth kill from behind became a throat cut, the through-door kill no longer rattles the door and alerts nearby guards, and a few sword combos were extended. No missions or modes were added or removed.

It belongs in an issue about manipulation because the stealth is only half the game. The other half is a small political simulation that reacts to every job you take. If you liked the idea of Tenchu but wanted the missions to *matter* to each other, this is the closest thing on PS2.

## Utakata's war, in plain terms

You play Goh, called the Crow: an amnesiac survivor of the Asuka ninja clan, which was wiped out in a single night. Pieces of his memory are scattered as glowing soul stones. A correspondent calling himself Onji sends advice by arrow to Goh's shack, and from that shack you take work.

Three powers are fighting over the province of Utakata at the end of the Muromachi period:

- **The Ichijo clan**, under Lord Nobuteru Ichijo at Utakata Castle. Legitimate, but short of money.
- **The Akame clan**, under Kagetora Akame at Fudo Castle. Rich from trade with Europeans, and the strongest militarily.
- **The Amurita sect**, a religious movement led by the charismatic Sadame from Sotai Tower.

Each side posts contracts. The official name for the system that resolves them was the "Harakiri Engine", and fan documentation describes how it pushes the war around:

- Finishing a lord's job raises his trust in you and damages whoever the job targeted.
- Being seen by a rival faction while you work for someone else lowers that rival's trust.
- Stealing a faction's rice weakens it and leaves its soldiers **hungry**. Hungry guards are easier to lure with poisoned food on later jobs.
- If a faction gets hold of stolen secret documents, its soldiers learn new combat moves. Stolen weapon plans give them bows, rapid-fire bows or guns.
- Lords can be assassinated, though Akame is said to use body doubles.

So a theft job is never just a theft job. It moves the front line, changes which enemies you'll meet next, and decides whose letters stop arriving. The campaign runs on in-game days; one FAQ writer finished in about 60.

There are three normal endings, one for each lord who is still standing. A hidden ending needs all three lords' trust kept roughly level, so you must play them off against each other. That's the "unscripted" part of this issue's title: the overall plot is fixed, but which side wins, and when, is yours to arrange.

## What you actually do on a contract

Fan FAQs list over a dozen contract types, from reconnaissance, theft and assassination to hauling crates of rice, guarding a lord, duels, kidnapping and wrecking an armoured oxcart. Optional conditions raise the fee: kill no one, stay unseen, or finish inside five minutes.

On the ground the game will feel familiar if you know Tenchu, but not identical:

- **Awareness.** Every nearby guard gets a pair of eyes at the top of the screen. White means unaware, purple means suspicious, red means he has seen you and orange means he is searching. A compass shows the direction of the nearest enemy. There's no Tenchu-style ki number.
- **Stealth kills** use Triangle: from behind, from a corner or ledge, from above, through a paper screen, by drowning, or on a guard who has stooped to take bait. Bodies can be dumped down wells or into rivers.
- **Physics and tools.** Spike promoted that objects have weight: stack crates to climb a wall, or knock a bucket down with a shuriken to make noise. The shop adds grappling hooks, caltrops, land mines, wind-up decoys and poisoned sushi. One fan report says guards learn to spot traps you overuse.
- **Alchemy.** Herbs, mushrooms and geckos go into a pot, and the result is bottled to drink, packed in a sphere to throw, or stuffed into sushi for a guard to eat.
- **The garden.** The yard behind Goh's hut holds objects carried home from missions. Raiders try to rob it, so in practice it's a defensive project.

There's also a mission editor descended from Tenchu's, with about 40 staff-made missions on the disc. A Japan-only expansion disc, *Shinobido Takumi*, collected over a hundred more.

## The first session

The game opens with a playable prologue that teaches the core stealth kills, followed by tutorial videos. After that Onji's letter points you at Utakata Castle, and the game settles into its real shape: a hideout menu of missions, letters, results, shop, garden and save. PALGN counted about six contracts on offer at a time. Alchemy only unlocks after your first visit to Asuka Village, so don't go looking for it in the first hour.

Failure here is a tax, not a reset. Most failed contracts cost you that mission's loot and some respect, and the game only ends outright in story battles. That makes it cheap to experiment: try the risky approach, see what the war does, and take the next job.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** This is the most systemic game in the issue. Contracts feed a faction model, the faction model changes the enemies, and the alchemy pot lets you improvise. Taking jobs for whoever pays, then quietly undermining them for a rival, produces the kind of emergent mischief roguelike players go looking for.

**Friction.** The 2006 reviews agree on the problems, and they're real:

- **The camera.** Eurogamer's Tom Bramwell called it "incontestably awful", and CNET Australia found it still sticks on scenery after an R1 re-centre.
- **Movement.** Eurogamer and PALGN found it effectively digital: you run or you stand. CNET found it skiddy, with accidental wall-runs.
- **Lock-on and items.** Item use while locked on means holding several buttons, and PALGN found the target hard to read.
- **Repetition.** CNET noted reused maps; Eurogamer singled out clunky crate-carrying jobs.

Expect the first couple of hours to be a fight with the controls before the systems open up.

## Controls and emulation: what's known, what isn't

The default layout, from a fan FAQ: sticks move and turn the camera, X jumps and wall-runs, held Circle sprints, R1 crouches and hugs walls, Square attacks, Triangle is the context action and stealth kill, L1 locks on, and L2 or R2 plus a face button picks one of eight quick items. Nothing needs pressure-sensitive buttons, which is good news on modern pads and handhelds, where analog face buttons are rare.

PCSX2's game database rates this exact European disc (SCES-53931) as perfect, with a built-in setting that fixes a hang when entering the garden; a libretro forum post reports that hang in the RetroArch PCSX2 core without it. **Nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** If you're setting it up in an emulator, play the prologue and then walk into the garden before committing to a campaign. If either fails, check the emulator's clamping options before blaming the disc.

## A first-session plan

- **Try Japanese voices with English subtitles.** Boomtown's reviewer recommended the original audio.
- **Learn three stealth kills in the prologue:** from behind, from a corner and from above.
- **Take one job from each lord before favouring any.** Read the letters afterwards to see how each side reacts.
- **Steal rice early,** then keep poisoned sushi for the hungry guards.
- **Save at the hideout before every contract.**

## How it was received

Release-era reviews of the PAL version liked the structure and tired of the mechanics:

- **Eurogamer**, Tom Bramwell, 3 August 2006: 7/10, with an honest verdict line: "Score-wise, it sounds like a 6, but I like it like a 7".
- **CNET Australia**, Randolph Ramsay, 20 July 2006: 6.8/10. Compelling structure, repetitive missions, loose controls.
- **PALGN**, 23 July 2006: 6.0/10. Fun rooftops; poor fog, camera and dub accents.
- **Boomtown**, Corey Brotherson, 22 September 2006: 7/10, praising the factions, kill variety and editor.

Some figures are only available second-hand. Metacritic excerpts show *Official PlayStation 2 Magazine UK* at 3/10 and *Edge* at 6/10. Wikipedia reports a Famitsu score of 34/40 for the Japanese release, but its source link is dead. Metacritic's normalised average is 59 from 15 critics. I found no IGN or GameSpot review of the PS2 game.

The follow-ups are different games. The PSP spin-off *Tales of the Ninja* drops the lord-and-contract system (Eurogamer gave it 3/10), and *Shinobido 2* on Vita is a sequel with a new hero.
