## Why this one: the game that taught a generation to count

This is the oldest game in the issue and the plainest statement of its idea: the best fight is one the guard never knows about. Acquire's *Tenchu* came out in Japan in February 1998, months before *Metal Gear Solid*, and it asks you to read a single gauge, choose a route over the rooftops, and strike once.

The edition this article follows is the North American *Tenchu: Stealth Assassins* (SLUS-00706). Redump catalogues two pressings: the original and a later "Rev 1", which is the Greatest Hits disc. Its main program carries the same build date as the original US disc, and no gameplay difference between them is documented. If you came to the series through *Tenchu 2* or *Wrath of Heaven*, this is a return visit: what the original does that its sequels smoothed over, and how it plays when the only controller it understands is a D-pad.

## Which Tenchu is which

This is one of the rare cases where the Western version is the more complete one. The Japanese original, *Rittai Ninja Katsugeki Tenchu*, published by Sony Music Entertainment, has eight stages. For the Activision release Acquire added two missions, **Cross the Checkpoint** and **Execute the Corrupt Minister**, bringing the total to ten. Game Informer's 1998 fact box noted "2 Exclusive to U.S. Release".

The changes went deeper than content:

- Every mission has **three enemy and item layouts**, chosen at random. The manual and a 1998 strategy guide both describe this.
- A GameSpot preview of the US build reported a frame rate fixed for up to five enemies on screen, guards who notice you on rooftops, and archers who shoot up at you.
- Acquire's lead programmer later wrote that stealth checks were made stricter for the overseas build, with **chain alerts** and **body discovery** retrofitted.
- There's a new ending film and bloodier combat.

Europe got the game in October 1998; in UK copies the shuriken became a "Crimson Blade" throwing knife, which *Play* blamed on weapons law. Japan received the Western build in 1999 as *Tenchu: Shinobi Gaisen*, with a hidden mission editor. Wikipedia credits an editor to the Western release too, but this issue couldn't confirm one on the US disc. The US street date is fuzzy: GameSpot and Metacritic say 31 August 1998, while IGN quoted Activision's producer in mid-September saying it would be in stores by the 16th.

So if you're choosing a disc: the US and European releases carry the ten-mission game, the Japanese original has eight, and *Shinobi Gaisen* is the Western content brought home with an editor on top.

## The loop, in plain terms

You pick Rikimaru, who is slower and tougher with a katana, or Ayame, who is faster and more fragile with two short blades. Then you pick a mission, hear the briefing, and go to the **Items screen**. The grappling hook always comes along. On top of that you may take up to four other item types, capped by a total count. Stock refills after each mission based on how well you did, so a good rating buys a better kit next time.

The tools are simple and mean:

- **Poison Rice** paralyses dogs and hungry guards who eat it.
- **Colored Rice** comes in five colours and works as a trail marker.
- **Caltrops** slow and injure pursuers.
- **Smoke bombs** blind anyone nearby. Mines, grenades and healing potions round out the basic list.

The heart of the game is the **ki meter**, the gauge at the lower left. In the screenshot at the top of this article it shows a red **!!**. The manual describes the symbols this way:

- **?** means someone nearby hasn't noticed you.
- **!** means he's seen something but hasn't worked out what. He might take you for a dog, a cat or a colleague.
- **!!** means you've been identified, and he will attack to kill.
- **!?** means he's searching, after a noise or after finding a body. Searching guards check the rooftops.

A number beside the meter climbs as an enemy gets closer to spotting you. The stealth kill is a one-press execution with Square from behind, and it only works on a target who is "entirely unaware", which means the meter can't read higher than **?**. Get it right and you see one of several cinematic kill animations. Get it wrong and you're in a clumsy sword fight.

Scoring makes the moral logic explicit. According to the manual, an unseen kill is worth 20 points, a detected kill 5, and killing an innocent costs you 30. Finishing without being spotted earns a 400-point bonus that shrinks each time you're seen. Your total ranks you as Thug, Novice, Ninja, Master Ninja or Grand Master. The 1998 strategy guide says the higher ranks unlock tools, and Grand Master on each mission awards a secret item. Fan lists name a Super Shuriken from Training, a Fire-Eater Scroll from mission 2 and a Chameleon Spell from mission 8, among others.

That's the reason to replay. A mission you've already cleared can still pay you something you can't get any other way.

## The first session

Training is an obstacle course of guards to kill unseen, worth doing once. Mission 1, *Punish the Evil Merchant*, comes with its own lesson: avoid the main gate, use the grappling hook, never harm the innocent. A fan guide's route is rooftops, attic, bodyguard, then the merchant Echigoya, who carries a gun. Mission 2 has you carry a message through a village of enemy ninja and dogs; mission 3 moves into forest and caves with pit traps, wolves and a bear.

It's night and foggy throughout. Wikipedia, citing Retro Gamer's making-of feature, says the night setting was a compromise between draw distance and frame rate. The constraint became atmosphere.

## Fit and friction

**Fit.** Tenchu is short, readable and replayable: ten missions, three layouts each, ranks you have to earn, and an item economy that rewards clean play. It's the purest version of "read the guard, pick the route, commit", and the clearest place to see where *Wrath of Heaven* and the later games got their rules.

**Friction.** Reviewers agreed on the same weaknesses in 1998, and they still apply:

- **The camera.** EGM named "Bad Camera" as its worst feature. *Next Generation* said the US version's camera was now locked to keep your character on screen, so it no longer swings round corners for you.
- **The controls.** Game Informer's Andy McNamara called them "quite clumsy". EGM's reviewer John R. wished for analog control and mid-mission saves.
- **No checkpoints.** If you die and continue, you lose every item except the hook. Missions are all or nothing, so set aside enough time to finish one.

The draw distance is short, and GameSpot noticed occasional pop-up. You'll often hear a guard before you see him.

## Controls and emulation: what's known, what isn't

The manual says plainly that the analog sticks are not supported. Movement is tank-style on the D-pad: up runs, down backs off, left and right turn, and double-tapping dashes or sidesteps. X jumps, Square attacks, Triangle uses the item, Circle or R1 crouches and flattens you against walls, L1 plus the D-pad looks around, and L2 and R2 cycle items.

DuckStation's game database lists the US disc (SLUS-00706) as having no known issues and notes that the analog sticks do nothing. On a modern pad or handheld, the D-pad is the controller; don't waste time binding the stick. **Nothing here was play-tested for this issue.** If you're setting it up, check that a mission loads and the D-pad drives movement, and check saving: the manual offers auto-save after each mission, and a 2024 fan guide says it's off by default.

## A first-session plan

- **Play Training once as Ayame, then mission 1 as Rikimaru.** Feel the difference in speed and toughness before committing.
- **Read the meter, not the screen.** If it shows **!**, stop and wait. Don't run.
- **Take Poison Rice and Smoke Bombs on mission 1.** Toss rice where a patrol will find it and watch what happens.
- **Aim for Ninja rank, not Grand Master.** Clean up later, once you know the layouts.
- **Replay mission 1 and watch for a different layout.** One fan guide says a retry often keeps the same layout and a reload can change it.

## How it was received

Tenchu was well received in the West, with constant reservations about the camera:

- **IGN**, Doug Perry, 18 September 1998: 9.0. He found it hard and short on aids, and summed it up: "Hard-core, smart, bloody, and fun as hell."
- **GameSpot**, Joe Fielder: 8.3. He praised the hook and kill animations. (The page's March 1998 date doesn't match the US release it reviews.)
- **Game Informer**, November 1998: 9. **Game Revolution**, September 1998: A.
- **Edge**, May 1998, Japanese import: 7/10, criticising the few enemy types and weak bosses.
- **Play** (UK): 77% for the PAL release.

Wikipedia reports an EGM average of 7.75 that couldn't be read in the scan consulted. Metacritic's normalised average is 87 from 14 critics. In a 2015 retrospective, the Guardian's Keith Stuart put it among the nine greatest stealth games, noting that "the tension and atmosphere still impress."
