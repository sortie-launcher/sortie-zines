## Why a snowboard game is here

SSX 3 usually gets filed under sports. That's fair if "sports" means simulation, but this plays more like a racing game with a resource meter. Tricks fill the meter, the meter becomes speed, and falling drains it, so every jump in a race asks whether the airtime is worth the boost. EA Canada's October 2003 sequel also swapped separate tracks for one connected mountain. Nothing here has been played; this draws on the US manual, two contemporary reviews and a 2004 fan guide.

## One mountain, three peaks

The manual promises "three expansive mountain peaks". Conquer the Mountain is the only mode that unlocks events and earns cash, and at the start only Peak 1 is open. The manual lists two kinds of event:

- **Race** events, with a qualifying round, semi-final and final.
- **Freestyle** events (Slopestyle, Super Pipe and Big Air), with a qualifying round and final.

GameSpot says a top-three finish out of six moves you on. Medal in every race event, or every freestyle event, on a peak and your rival calls you out to a one-on-one in the Backcountry. Beat them to unlock the Peak Race, a summit-to-city run the manual puts at 12 minutes to half an hour, or the Peak Jam, which chains several tracks.

Winning isn't the only way forward. Completing *any* Peak Goal opens the next peak: win the events, explore (clear over 30% of a peak's Big Challenges and collect over 30% of its crystals), or earn enough cash. A weak racer can still climb by exploring, and you can ride past any start gate to freeride.

## The adrenaline economy

The spiral on the right of the screen is the adrenaline meter. Tricks fill it, and Square spends it on the ground as boost. The fuller the meter, the bigger the boost: red beats orange, and orange beats yellow. Adrenaline drains if you stop doing tricks and drops when you fall. The manual's advice is to do tricks on the way to an event so you arrive already charged.

When the meter is full, tweaking a grab turns it into an **Uber** trick. Four Ubers spell U-B-E-R and unlock Super Ubers. Spell SUPER UBER and the meter stays full for a short time, which the manual calls "a massive speed advantage". IGN notes that SSX Tricky introduced Ubers; SSX 3 adds three tiers, and its meter steps down gradually instead of emptying.

Scoring penalises repetition: the same trick is worth less each time. A combo doubles every trick in it, with a bonus that grows the longer it runs, but falling mid-combo loses all of it. Board presses on the right stick keep a combo alive between jumps.

## Lines, shortcuts and falling

The manual says racing is about "finding the fastest line" and that Slopestyle has "no one proper line". Handplants, on Circle, are a route tool as well as a trick: they reach shortcuts, upper pipes and transitions. The fan guide warns that some shortcuts need a fully upgraded rider.

![A rider doing a one-handed handplant on a blue rail. SSX 3 promotional screenshot from IGN's October 2003 PS2 review page; the same gallery includes an Xbox menu screen, so the platform can't be identified from this frame. © Electronic Arts, via IGN](media/ssx3-mountain-lines-handplant.webp)

Falling costs little time. GameSpot's Greg Kasavin said the real loss is "a good chunk of your boost"; you also lose Uber access until the meter refills, plus any combo in progress. Kasavin found no rubber-banding in the CPU riders: a lead holds, and a bad fall stays costly. He also disliked invisible boundaries that reset you, while IGN's Douglass C. Perry hit spots where riders get stuck and drops with no warning. Select resets your rider manually.

## Cash, stats and the lodge

Cash comes from events, Big Challenges, crystals and freeride tricks. Lodges at the mid-mountain stations sell attributes, gear, Uber tricks, music and cheat-character skins. Every rider starts with "rock-bottom" ratings, and Perry notes that boards and clothes don't affect handling; only bought abilities do. The fan guide lists seven attributes (acceleration, edging, speed, spin, stability, toughness, tricks), each rated 1 to 11 and costlier at every level. It's a garage layer, but a shallow one: you choose what to buy first, with no trade-offs.

## Controls from the US manual

![The US manual's Complete Controls page (page 6) listing the Default configuration: Square for boost, tweak and wipeout recovery; L1, R1, L2 and R2 for grabs and pushes. SSX 3 NTSC-U PS2 manual, © Electronic Arts, scan via Video Game Museum](media/ssx3-mountain-lines-manual-controls.webp)

The Default configuration:

- Steer with the D-pad or left stick. Push up to build speed and down to check it.
- Hold X to crouch and release to jump. As the manual puts it, "The longer you crouch, the higher you fly."
- Hold a D-pad direction while crouched to pre-wind spins. In the air, the D-pad adds late spins.
- Square boosts on the ground and tweaks a held grab in the air. Tap it repeatedly to recover from a wipeout.
- L1, R1, L2 and R2 grab in the air and push on the ground. Press L1 and R1 together to block.
- Push the right stick up or down for board presses. Hold Circle and press a D-pad direction to handplant.

Both reviews say tapping jump recovers a mid-air bail; the manual doesn't, and it's unchecked. The Pro configuration moves spins to the left stick but blocks turning while crouched. The layout uses all four shoulder buttons plus the right stick, so on a modern pad, check where each one lands before the first race.

## Your first 20 minutes

- Start Conquer the Mountain on Peak 1. Single Event only offers events you've already unlocked.
- Do small tricks while riding to the first race so you start with some adrenaline.
- Enter whichever Peak 1 race is open. The fan guide lists Snow Jam and Metro City Breakdown, with heat target times of about three minutes. Aim for third place.
- Do one grab per jump and land it. Boost on straights once the meter is orange or red.
- If the Big Air event at Crows Nest is open, use it to practise pre-winds and tweaks without having to pick a line.
- If a run goes wrong, use Restart from the pause menu.
- Spend your first cash on Stability. The fan guide says it makes landings more forgiving. This is my suggestion, not a tested recommendation.

## Fit, friction and editions

**Fit:** events are short and restart instantly, risk is easy to read from the meter, and the depth comes from learning lines. There's no long tutorial. **Friction:** Kasavin compared the forgiving resets to "an interactive roller-coaster ride", found the Big Challenges weaker than the main events, and said gold in everything is hard. Perry calls Peak 3 "brutal". Solo races are against CPU riders; GameSpot describes a two-player vertical split-screen mode, and Wikipedia says the PS2 online service has been shut down. The licensed EA Radio Big soundtrack comes with DJ chatter, but IGN says you can switch to ambient sound or a custom playlist.

This article follows the USA PS2 disc. Redump lists the original and Greatest Hits under one serial, SLUS-20772. The upstream desktop PCSX2 GameIndex rates SLUS-20772 at compatibility 5, with graphics fixes for lighting and depth lines; that is upstream data, not a tested result. The Xbox and GameCube versions ran at a higher resolution (GameSpot), the GameCube pad lacked buttons (Perry), and the Game Boy Advance version is a separate game by Visual Impact.

## Reception in 2003

GameSpot's Kasavin gave it 9.0 on October 21, 2003, in a review covering the PS2, Xbox and GameCube versions. He praised how the music reacts to your riding but disliked the hidden out-of-bounds areas. Perry's IGN review of the PS2 version gave it 9.5 and an Editors' Choice on October 17, 2003. His complaints were riders getting stuck and rails too high to catch from the side. Metacritic's aggregate for the PS2 version is 93 from 41 reviews. The 2018 Xbox One backward-compatibility release came much later and isn't this disc.
