## A fighting game with a frisbee

Windjammers gets filed under sports, which undersells it. Data East's 1994 Neo Geo game (Flying Power Disc in Japan, released there on February 17, 1994) is a one-on-one duel. You defend half a walled court against one opponent, playing as one of six characters with different speed and power. Curves use half-circle stick motions, supers are charged, and a super can be reversed if you press at exactly the right moment. Hardcore Gamer's 2017 review found it "very reminiscent of the fighting games" of its day, which is why it's in this issue. The decisions come from spacing and reads, not from sports knowledge.

## The court, the clock and the zones

Each goal runs the width of the end wall and is split into three zones: yellow scores 3 and red scores 5. A disc that falls uncaught gives the thrower 2. BakaOrochi's FAQ adds one more way to score: throw hard enough at a defender standing near their goal and you push them into it.

At default settings a set ends at 12 points or after 30 seconds. If time runs out the leader takes the set, and a tie gives the set to both players. Two sets win the match; a deadlock goes to sudden death.

The Neo-Geo.com wiki and the FAQ broadly agree on the six courts:

- **Beach:** red centre, yellow ends, similar sizes.
- **Lawn:** red centre, about a quarter of the goal.
- **Tiled:** reversed, with red at the ends.
- **Concrete:** tiny red ends, two barriers near the side walls.
- **Clay:** red centre, two barriers near the middle.
- **Stadium:** the red zone starts small and grows on the side of whoever keeps getting scored on.

That Stadium rule is a built-in comeback mechanic. The FAQ says CPU matches use the courts in a fixed order, finishing at the Stadium.

## Holding the disc

After a catch you can't move, and the longer you hold the disc the slower your throw, so you have to decide at once:

- **A** throws straight. **Direction + A** angles the throw so it can bank off the walls.
- A **half-circle + A** curves the throw. The Windjammers France wiki says Mita's throws curve hard and Wessel's barely curve.
- **B** lobs, and the stick picks where it lands. The France wiki says an instant lob lands deep and a delayed one lands near the net. An uncaught lob scores 2, but an opponent who reaches the landing spot in time gets a free charge.
- If you throw almost the instant you catch, you get a "supersonic" shot that leaves a trail. The France wiki says the timing window is wider when you're behind on points, a second comeback mechanic.

## Defence, the reticle and reversals

Off the disc, **Direction + A** slides; hold A to slide further, at the cost of a longer recovery. You must face an incoming disc to catch it: one that reaches you from behind knocks you down.

Press **A** with no direction just as the disc arrives and you block it into the air, with a reticle marking where it will land. Stand under the reticle and your character glows and charges. Older guides say to tap A while charging, but the France wiki says the charge is automatic. Then you have three options: A fires your character's special, a curve motion plus A throws a spinning or bouncing variant, and B launches a super lob that rolls into the goal. If a super is coming at you, pressing as it reaches you sends it back, and Arcade Quartermaster says each reversal returns faster.

The block is where the real decision sits. According to the FAQ, blocking early sends the disc farther away. That gives you time to reach it, but every charge is telegraphed, so it also gives your opponent time to read your special. A plain catch gives up the super in exchange for an immediate throw.

## Six players, three tiers

![The English character select screen, with speed/power bars and Data East's own labels: H. Mita and B. Yoo "for BEGINNER", J. Costa and L. Biaggi "for MEDIUM", G. Scott and K. Wessel "for EXPERT". The game ranks its own cast by difficulty:

- **Hiromi Mita (Japan):** walks fastest, throws weakest.
- **Jordi Costa (Spain) and Loris Biaggi (Italy):** the middle pair.
- **Gary Scott (USA):** throws hardest.
- **Klaus Wessel (Germany):** walks slowest; the France wiki says he also charges fastest.

The sixth player is Beeho Yoo of South Korea or Steve Miller of Britain: the same sprite with a different name and voice. Sources disagree on which region gets which. The FAQ author also disputes the official ratings. In his view Mita's speed makes beginners overshoot, so he suggests starting with Biaggi or Costa.

## Your first 20 minutes

- Start a one-player game as **Biaggi**. No sports rules are needed: just get the disc past the other player.
- In the first match, don't slide. Walk to the disc, and throw with A the moment you catch it, away from where the CPU is standing.
- Then try one block per rally: tap A with no direction just before the disc reaches you, walk to the reticle, and fire the special.
- Lob with B whenever the CPU comes up to the net.
- Try a curve: back, down, forward, then A. Watch which way it bends.
- After match two comes Dog Distance, a bonus game you can't lose. Stop there or at your first loss, and note which court beat you.

## Fit and friction

Matches are short. Hardcore Gamer timed two or three sets at "about five minutes or less", and there's almost nothing to learn before you can play. It is one of the easiest games in this issue to try in a spare quarter of an hour.

The friction comes later. Solo play is a single arcade ladder across the six courts, with Dog Distance and disc bowling in between, and Hardcore Gamer says the ladder includes a clone of your own character. According to the FAQ, difficulty runs from 1 to 8 with 4 as standard, continues are on, and the CPU plays its "beginner" characters as well as the experts. Losing only costs a retry, but it repeats quickly. In 1994 the German magazine *Video Games* scored the game 71% for one player and 90% for two. In 2018 Nintendo Life was still warning solo players.

Local two-player needs two controllers on one machine. The original arcade game has no built-in online play; DotEmu's later edition adds it. Emulator netplay is a separate possibility, not tested for this issue.

## Controls and editions

The original uses an 8-way stick and two buttons: **A** throws, catches, blocks and slides, and **B** lobs. The game's own how-to screen shows the C and D buttons crossed out, and the MAME history entry agrees. I couldn't find a scan of the original manual. Nothing here was playtested; on a D-pad, check that half-circle motions register reliably before judging the curve shots.

Home versions followed: AES cartridges (Japan on April 8, 1994, and the US the same year) and Neo Geo CD in 1995. DotEmu's 2017 PS4/Vita reissue added online play and menu access to the bonus games. That is a later edition, not the original arcade release this article describes.

## Reception, then and later

Release-era coverage is thin. Wolfgang Schaedle of *Video Games* (June 1994) gave 71% solo and 90% two-player, saying that anyone playing mainly alone would have less fun than with a partner. *Ultimate Future Games* #5 (April 1995) gave 73% to the Japanese-titled Flying Power Disc and called it "a welcome alternative to the beat-em-ups". Wikipedia lists about a dozen more scores, among them Edge 5/10 and Famitsu 28/40, which I couldn't check against scans. Hardcore Gamer says EGM and GamePro never properly reviewed it.

Later reassessments were warmer. Hardcore Gamer gave the DotEmu release 4/5 (PS4), Gamecritics gave it 7/10 (PS4) and Nintendo Life 8/10 (Switch). Nintendo Life said the original "launched to lukewarm reviews".
